NYSC to First Job: How to Turn Your CV From Corps Member to Candidate

September 2026 · 6 min read

One of the most common frustrations fresh graduates and corps members run into is a CV that looks thin next to candidates with years of paid work experience — even when the underlying work (a PPA placement, a final-year project, a SIWES attachment) genuinely demonstrates real, relevant skill. The problem is usually framing, not substance.

Key takeaways
  • NYSC and PPA work should be written like a job, with specific responsibilities and outcomes — not just listed as a mandatory program.
  • International or unfamiliar recruiters may not know what "NYSC" means — a short clarifying phrase helps.
  • School projects, SIWES attachments, and volunteer work all count as real experience when described with specifics.
  • A skills section carries more weight for fresh graduates than it does for experienced candidates — don't skip it.

Write your PPA placement like a real job

A common mistake is listing NYSC as one flat line: "National Youth Service Corps, 2024–2025." Instead, treat your Primary Place of Assignment the way you'd describe any job — job title (or closest equivalent), organization, dates, and 2-3 bullet points on what you actually did and achieved there. "Assisted with admin tasks" is weak; "Managed student records for 200+ pupils and reduced filing errors by digitizing the record system" is a real accomplishment, if that's genuinely closer to what you did.

Explain NYSC for readers who don't know what it is

If you're applying to a multinational, a remote role, or any recruiter outside Nigeria, "NYSC" may mean nothing to them. A short clarifying phrase — "National Youth Service Corps (mandatory one-year national service program for Nigerian graduates)" — removes the confusion instantly, without taking up much space.

Your final-year project and SIWES attachment count as experience

A significant academic project or an industrial training (SIWES) placement is real, describable work — treat it the same way as a job entry. Name the project, what you built or analyzed, the tools or skills involved, and any measurable outcome, even a modest one ("Built a small inventory tracker used by the department for stock records").

Don't skip the skills section

For someone with limited paid work history, a clear skills section does more work than it does for a senior candidate — it's often the fastest way for a recruiter to see what you can actually do. List specific, genuine skills (a software tool, a language, a technical competency) rather than vague soft-skill claims like "hardworking" or "team player," which every CV says and few recruiters weigh heavily.

Volunteer work and student leadership count too. Leading a student association, organizing a community event, or consistent volunteer work all demonstrate real skills (coordination, communication, initiative) — describe them with the same specificity as any other experience entry, not as an afterthought.

Keep it to one page if you can

With limited work history, a one-page CV is usually the right length — stretching thin experience across two pages with excessive white space or repeated phrasing tends to look worse than a tight, well-organized single page.

Frequently asked questions

Should NYSC go under "Experience" or its own section?
Under Experience, treated the same as any other role — this keeps your CV's structure standard and easy for both a human reader and any ATS software to follow.

What if my PPA work wasn't related to my career goal?
Focus on transferable skills (organization, communication, problem-solving, tools used) rather than the specific subject matter — most PPA placements involve at least some skills that carry over to other roles.

Is it okay to have a CV with no formal work experience at all?
Yes — lead with education, projects, and skills instead, and let a strong summary at the top frame what you bring even without a long work history.
