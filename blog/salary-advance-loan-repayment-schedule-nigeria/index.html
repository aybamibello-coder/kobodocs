Salary Advance & Cooperative Loan Repayment Schedules in Nigeria

August 2026 · 7 min read

Salary advances and cooperative (thrift society) loans run on trust more than paperwork in a lot of Nigerian workplaces and cooperatives — which is exactly how disputes start. "I thought I'd finished paying" and "you still owe two more installments" are both said with total confidence when there's no written schedule both sides agreed to upfront.

A repayment schedule fixes this before it becomes a problem. Here's how to build one properly.

Key takeaways
  • A repayment schedule breaks a loan into fixed installments, showing exactly how much of each payment goes to principal vs. interest.
  • Flat-rate interest (a fixed percentage of the original amount, unchanged throughout) is the most common method for salary advances and cooperative loans — simpler to calculate, though it costs the borrower more overall than reducing-balance interest.
  • Most disputes come from an unclear schedule, not a bad-faith borrower — write it down before the first payment, not after a disagreement starts.
  • Deducting repayments directly from salary is common for staff advances, but should still be documented with the employee's written agreement.
  • A missed installment should have an agreed consequence stated upfront — extend the schedule, adjust the next payment, or apply a late fee — not decided in the moment.

Flat-rate vs. reducing-balance interest

Most salary advances and cooperative/thrift loans in Nigeria use flat-rate interest: a fixed percentage of the original loan amount, applied for the full term, regardless of how much principal has already been repaid. It's simple to calculate and explain, which is exactly why informal lenders and cooperatives favor it.

The alternative, reducing-balance interest, calculates interest only on the remaining principal each period — as the loan gets paid down, the interest portion shrinks. This is more common with formal bank loans and is cheaper for the borrower overall, but it's more complex to calculate by hand.

Why this matters: a ₦200,000 loan at 10% flat rate over 5 months costs a fixed ₦20,000 in interest regardless of how the repayments are structured. The same loan under reducing-balance interest would cost noticeably less, since interest is charged only on what's still outstanding each month. Know which method your cooperative or employer uses before agreeing to a rate.

A worked example: flat-rate salary advance

Say an employee takes a ₦150,000 salary advance, to be repaid over 5 months at a flat 8% total interest:

  • Interest: 8% × ₦150,000 = ₦12,000
  • Total repayable: ₦150,000 + ₦12,000 = ₦162,000
  • Monthly installment: ₦162,000 ÷ 5 = ₦32,400

The schedule should show all five installments upfront, with dates, so both the employer (or cooperative) and the borrower can check off each payment against the same document — not two different mental tallies.

What a proper repayment schedule should include

  • Loan amount and interest rate (and whether it's flat or reducing-balance)
  • Total repayable amount, including interest
  • Number of installments and the amount of each one
  • Due date for every installment, not just the final one
  • How payments will be made — salary deduction, bank transfer, cash at the cooperative meeting
  • What happens if an installment is missed — this needs to be agreed before it happens, not decided in the moment when it does

Salary deductions specifically: get written agreement first

Deducting a loan repayment directly from an employee's salary is common practice for staff advances, but it should always be backed by the employee's written agreement to the deduction — not just a verbal understanding when the advance was given. This protects the employer from a wage-dispute claim and gives the employee a clear record of exactly how much is being deducted and until when.

Cooperative and thrift society loans

Cooperative and thrift (Ajo/Esusu-adjacent) societies often lend to members based on their contribution history, at rates the group sets internally — commonly a flat percentage over a fixed number of months. The same principle applies: write the schedule down and share it with the borrower before the first payment, rather than relying on the group's collective memory of who's paid what. Common mistakes

  • No written schedule at all — relying on memory is the single biggest cause of "I thought I'd finished" disputes.
  • Interest rate not clearly stated as flat or reducing-balance — leads to genuine confusion about how much is actually owed partway through.
  • Uneven installments with no explanation — if a later payment is meant to be smaller or larger, say so upfront in the schedule.
  • No agreed consequence for a missed payment — deciding this after the fact, under pressure, rarely goes well for either side.
  • Deducting from salary without documented consent.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between flat-rate and reducing-balance interest?
Flat-rate interest is calculated once on the original loan amount and stays fixed for the whole term. Reducing-balance interest is recalculated each period based on what's still outstanding, so the interest portion shrinks as the loan is paid down — it's cheaper for the borrower but more complex to calculate.

Can an employer deduct a salary advance directly from wages in Nigeria?
Yes, this is common practice, but it should be backed by the employee's written agreement to the deduction and its schedule, not just a verbal understanding when the advance was given.

How is interest usually calculated on cooperative society loans in Nigeria?
Most cooperative and thrift societies use a flat rate — a fixed percentage of the original loan amount, applied for the full repayment term — since it's simple to calculate and explain to members.

What should happen if someone misses a loan installment?
The consequence — whether that's extending the schedule, adjusting the next payment, or a late fee — should be agreed and written into the schedule before the loan starts, not decided in the moment a payment is missed.

Do I need a lawyer to create a loan repayment schedule?
No — a repayment schedule is a straightforward calculation, not a legal contract in itself, though for larger or more formal loans you may also want a separate loan agreement document alongside it.
