Affidavit in Nigeria: Name Change, Age Declaration & Loss of Document Explained

July 2026 · 8 min read

At some point, almost every Nigerian adult needs an affidavit — for a name that changed at marriage, a missing birth certificate that's now blocking a passport application, or a lost school certificate that needs replacing. It's one of the most common legal documents in the country, and also one of the most misunderstood: people aren't always sure what it needs to say, where it has to be sworn, or when a newspaper publication is actually required alongside it.

This guide breaks down the three most common affidavit types — name change, age declaration, and loss of document — plus the newspaper publication step that trips people up specifically for name changes.

Key takeaways
  • An affidavit only becomes a valid legal document once it's sworn before a Commissioner for Oaths, a court registrar, or a notary public — a drafted-but-unsworn affidavit has no legal effect.
  • A name change affidavit almost always needs to be followed by a newspaper publication in a national daily — this is what makes the name change a matter of public record, not just a private declaration.
  • An age declaration affidavit is the standard route for anyone without an original birth certificate — common for older Nigerians, and often required for NIN, passport, or pension applications.
  • A loss of document affidavit typically needs to reference a police report where the loss involves anything security-sensitive (ATM card, particular IDs), though this varies by what was lost.
  • Who drafts the affidavit matters far less than getting it correctly sworn — a self-prepared affidavit is just as valid as a lawyer-drafted one, once it's properly sworn.

What an affidavit actually is

An affidavit is a written statement of fact that you swear to be true, under oath, before someone legally authorized to administer that oath — typically a Commissioner for Oaths at a High Court registry, a Magistrate court, or a notary public. It becomes a legal document specifically because of that sworn step, not because of who typed it up. Making a false statement in a sworn affidavit is perjury, which is exactly why the process carries legal weight — courts, banks, and government agencies treat a sworn affidavit as a serious declaration, not a formality.

Name change affidavit — and why newspaper publication matters

A name change affidavit is most commonly needed after marriage (adopting a spouse's surname), correcting a long-standing misspelling across official documents, or a deliberate personal or religious name change. The affidavit itself states your former name, your new name, and your declaration that you now wish to be known by the new name for all purposes.

What catches a lot of people off guard: for the name change to be recognized by banks, schools, and government agencies, it generally needs to be followed by a publication in a national newspaper — a short public notice stating that you (former name) now go by (new name). This publication step is what makes the change a matter of public record rather than just a private sworn statement, and many institutions specifically ask to see both the affidavit and the newspaper cutting together.

Keep both documents together: When a bank, school, or government office asks for proof of a name change, they typically want the sworn affidavit and the newspaper publication as a pair — one without the other is often treated as incomplete.

Age declaration affidavit

Not everyone in Nigeria has an original birth certificate, particularly older adults born before formal birth registration was widespread in their area. An age declaration affidavit fills that gap — a sworn statement declaring your date of birth (or, where the exact date genuinely isn't known, your age as at a stated date), often supported by the recollection of a parent or older relative where possible. This is commonly required for NIN registration, international passport applications, pension processing, and sometimes school admission where an official birth certificate isn't available.

Loss of document affidavit

When an original certificate, ID card, or similar document is lost, misplaced, or stolen, a loss of document affidavit is what most institutions require before they'll issue a replacement or accept your claim that the original no longer exists. The affidavit should state clearly what was lost, roughly when and how (to the extent known), and that despite reasonable effort, the document hasn't been found. Where the loss involves anything security-sensitive — a lost ATM card, means of identification, or similar — it's worth first filing a report at the nearest police station, since some institutions will ask for the police report reference alongside the affidavit itself.

Where affidavits are sworn, and what it costs

Affidavits are sworn at a High Court registry (before a Commissioner for Oaths), a Magistrate court, or before a notary public. The process is generally quick — you present the drafted affidavit, the Commissioner or notary confirms your identity and that you understand the content, you sign in their presence, and they countersign and stamp it. Court and notary fees for this are typically modest, usually a few thousand naira, though this can vary by state and by court.

Do you need a lawyer to draft an affidavit?

No — an affidavit doesn't need to be lawyer-drafted to be valid. What actually matters is that the content is accurate and complete, and that it's properly sworn before an authorized officer. Many Nigerians draft their own affidavit (or use a template) and simply have it sworn at the registry. A lawyer becomes more useful for unusual or high-stakes situations — a complex name change history, or an affidavit that's likely to be contested — rather than for the standard cases covered here.

Common affidavit mistakes

  • Inconsistent name spelling across the affidavit and your other official documents — even a single-letter difference can cause problems later.
  • Treating an unsworn document as valid — a printed affidavit that hasn't actually been sworn before a Commissioner or notary has no legal standing, no matter how official it looks.
  • Skipping the newspaper publication for a name change, then being surprised when a bank or school won't accept the affidavit alone.
  • Vague statements — "lost my certificate sometime last year" is weaker than a specific, clear declaration of what happened.
  • Not keeping copies — always keep multiple certified copies once an affidavit is sworn; replacing a lost affidavit means going through the entire sworn process again.

Frequently asked questions

Is a self-drafted affidavit valid in Nigeria?
Yes. What makes an affidavit legally valid is that it's properly sworn before a Commissioner for Oaths, court registrar, or notary public — not who originally drafted the wording.

Do I always need a newspaper publication for a name change?
It's generally expected for the change to be recognized by banks, schools, and government agencies, since it's what puts the change on public record. Some smaller or purely internal uses may not strictly require it, but most formal institutions will ask for it.

What newspapers are accepted for a name change publication?
Any recognized national daily is generally accepted — the requirement is that it's a genuine, dated publication in a newspaper of public circulation, not a specific title.

Can I get an age declaration affidavit if I have no idea of my exact birth date?
Yes — where the exact date genuinely isn't known, the affidavit can declare your age as at a stated date based on your best knowledge and the recollection of family members, rather than requiring a precise date you don't have.

Do I need a police report for every loss of document affidavit?
Not always — it depends on what was lost. Security-sensitive items like ATM cards or identification documents commonly require a police report reference, while other lost certificates may not.
