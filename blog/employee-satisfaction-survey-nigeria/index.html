Employee Satisfaction Surveys: How Nigerian Companies Get Honest Answers

September 2026 · 6 min read

The hardest part of an employee satisfaction survey isn't writing the questions — it's getting staff to answer them honestly. In many Nigerian workplaces, a visible hierarchy and fear of being identified means employees often give safe, positive answers regardless of how they actually feel, unless the survey is genuinely structured to protect their identity.

Key takeaways
  • Anonymity has to be real and communicated clearly, not just claimed — employees need to trust it before they'll answer honestly.
  • Shorter, more frequent "pulse" surveys (5-10 questions, monthly or quarterly) work better than one long annual survey.
  • Ask about specific things (workload, management communication, growth opportunities) rather than a single vague "are you happy" question.
  • Sharing what changed as a result of the survey is what makes employees answer honestly the next time.

Why employees give dishonest answers on satisfaction surveys

Two things usually cause this: employees don't believe the survey is truly anonymous, or a previous survey didn't lead to any visible change, so they've concluded it's not worth the risk of being honest. Both are fixable, but require deliberate effort — not just adding "this survey is anonymous" to the top of the form.

How to make anonymity credible

  • Don't collect names or emails — if you need to track responses over time, use a random participant code instead of identifying information.
  • Avoid identifying combinations of questions. Asking department, role, and tenure together in a small team can make an individual identifiable even without their name.
  • Have someone outside direct management review results — HR or a third party, not the employee's direct supervisor, especially for smaller teams.
  • Share aggregate results, not individual responses — and say so upfront, so employees know what "anonymous" actually means in practice.

What to actually ask

Skip a single "Are you satisfied at work?" question — it's too broad to act on. Break it into specific areas instead:

  • Workload and work-life balance
  • Clarity of communication from management
  • Opportunities for growth or skill development
  • Whether they feel their work is recognized
  • One open-text question: "What's one thing that would make your job better?"

Pulse surveys vs. one big annual survey

A single long annual survey often suffers from fatigue — employees rush through it, and by the time results are analyzed, the moment they were reflecting on has passed. A shorter "pulse" survey (5-10 questions) run monthly or quarterly gives you a more current, trackable signal, and it's a smaller ask each time, which tends to improve honesty and completion rate.

The step most companies skip: telling employees what changed because of their feedback. Even a short internal note — "based on last quarter's survey, we've adjusted X" — is what convinces staff the next survey is worth answering honestly.

Frequently asked questions

How many employees do I need before a survey stays anonymous?
There's no fixed number, but in very small teams (under 5-10 people), even a fully anonymous survey can be identifiable by writing style or specific complaints — consider a verbal check-in format instead for very small teams.

Should managers see individual responses?
Generally no — aggregate results by team or department are safer for maintaining trust, with individual comments reviewed only by HR or leadership removed from direct reporting lines.

How often should we run an employee satisfaction survey?
A short pulse survey monthly or quarterly, with a more detailed annual review, tends to work better than relying on one long survey a year.
