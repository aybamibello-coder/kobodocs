Is an e-Signature Legally Valid in Nigeria?

August 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer: yes. If you've ever hesitated to send a contract for electronic signature because you weren't sure it would hold up, or printed, signed, and scanned a document out of habit rather than necessity, it's worth knowing exactly what the law actually says — because it's more settled than most people assume.

Key takeaways
  • Section 93 of the Evidence Act 2011 and the Companies and Allied Matters Act 2020 both recognise electronic signatures as legally binding.
  • What matters legally isn't the visual style of the signature — it's whether it can be reliably tied to the person who signed.
  • A proper audit trail (timestamp, IP address, what was shown to the signer) is what makes a signature defensible if it's ever disputed.
  • Signers don't need their own account or software — a private link they open and sign in their browser is enough.

Where this actually comes from in the law

Two pieces of Nigerian law do the heavy lifting here. Section 93 of the Evidence Act 2011 sets out how electronic signatures can be admitted as evidence — broadly, if a signature (electronic or otherwise) can be shown to have been made by the person it's attributed to, it's treated as valid. Separately, the Companies and Allied Matters Act 2020 (CAMA) — the same law that governs company registration, filings, and increasingly digital corporate processes — recognises electronic execution of documents in a way that lines up with this.

Neither law requires a specific software vendor, a specific visual signature style, or a notarised electronic certificate for an ordinary business contract. What they require is attribution: being able to show the signature came from the person it claims to be from.

What actually makes a signature defensible

If a signed document is ever disputed — someone claims they never agreed to it, or claims someone else signed on their behalf — what matters isn't how the signature looks. A scribbled squiggle and a typed name can carry equal weight, or equally little, depending on the evidence around them. What actually matters:

  • A timestamp — exactly when the signature was made.
  • An IP address — where the signing happened from, at the moment it happened.
  • A record of what was shown — the exact document the signer saw and agreed to, not a version that could have been altered afterward.

Together, this forms an audit trail. It's the same logic email delivery receipts or bank transaction logs rely on — not proof beyond all doubt, but a reasonable, contemporaneous record that's far stronger than "they said they signed it."

What a signature style does and doesn't prove. A hand-drawn signature on a screen looks more familiar and can feel more convincing, but legally it isn't inherently stronger than a typed name — the audit trail around it is what carries the actual weight.

Does the person signing need an account?

No, and this is one of the more common misconceptions. A properly built e-signature tool sends each signer a private, unique link — they open it, review the document, sign directly in their browser, and that's it. No software to install, no account to create. This matters practically: asking a client or contractor to sign up for a new platform just to approve one document adds friction that often causes delay, exactly the thing e-signature is supposed to remove.

When you might still want a physical signature

Electronic signatures cover the overwhelming majority of everyday business documents — contracts, invoices, quotations, agreements. A small number of document types (certain land transactions, some documents required for specific government or court processes) may still call for a wet signature, notarisation, or particular formalities depending on the specific requirement involved. When in doubt for a high-stakes or unusual document, it's worth confirming with a lawyer rather than assuming either way.

Frequently asked questions

Is an e-signature the same as a scanned signature image?
Not quite. A scanned image of a signature pasted onto a document has no audit trail behind it — no timestamp, no attribution. A proper e-signature process captures the surrounding evidence, not just the image.

Can I still print and hand-sign if I prefer?
Yes — nothing about e-signature being valid removes the option of a physical signature. It's an alternative, not a requirement.

What if the other party refuses to sign electronically?
Some people are simply more comfortable with a physical signature, and that's a reasonable preference to accommodate — there's no legal obligation on either party to sign electronically if they'd rather not.
