How to Create a Professional Invoice in Nigeria: A Complete Guide

July 2026 · 12 min read

An invoice is the difference between "I'll pay you when I remember" and a client who actually pays on time. If you run a business in Nigeria — whether that's a fashion line in Lekki, a consulting outfit in Abuja, or a one-person freelance operation — a clear, professional invoice is one of the cheapest things you can do to get paid faster and look more credible doing it.

This guide walks through exactly what a Nigerian invoice should include, the mistakes that make clients stall on payment, and how to put one together in under a minute.

Key takeaways
  • Every invoice needs a sequential, never-reused invoice number, a due date, and itemized line entries.
  • VAT (7.5%) is added to your subtotal first; WHT, where it applies, is deducted afterward — the order matters.
  • You don't need a registered company to invoice — a consistent trading name and bank details are enough.
  • Clear payment terms and upfront bank details get you paid faster than a vague "please pay soon".
  • Quote → invoice → receipt is the correct order for tracking a sale from proposal to proof of payment.

What every Nigerian invoice needs

At minimum, a proper invoice should include:

  • Your business name and contact details — even if you're not yet a registered company, use a consistent trading name.
  • A unique invoice number — sequential (INV-0001, INV-0002...), never reused. This matters more than most people think — see below.
  • Invoice date and due date — "net 7", "net 14", or a specific date. Ambiguity here is where late payment starts.
  • The client's name — who you're billing, clearly.
  • An itemized description — what was supplied, quantity, unit price, and line total. "Consulting services" is weaker than "Website redesign — homepage & 4 inner pages".
  • Subtotal, VAT (where it applies), and the final total due.
  • How to pay — bank name, account number, account name, or a Paystack/payment link. Don't make a client hunt for your account details in a separate WhatsApp message.

Why invoice numbering actually matters

It's tempting to skip a formal numbering system when you're small — but a sequential invoice number does two things: it makes your business look established, and it gives you (and your accountant, eventually) a clean paper trail. If a client disputes a payment six months later, "show me invoice INV-0047" is a much easier conversation than digging through old chat messages. Start at INV-0001 and never renumber or reuse a number, even for a cancelled invoice — just note it as void and move to the next number.

Where VAT and WHT fit in

Most invoices between a business and a paying company need to account for VAT (currently 7.5%), and sometimes Withholding Tax (WHT) as well, depending on the type of service. These two work in opposite directions — VAT is added to what you charge, WHT is deducted from what you're paid — and getting the order wrong is one of the most common invoicing mistakes in Nigeria.

Quick rule of thumb: VAT is added to your subtotal first. WHT (where it applies) is then deducted from that VAT-inclusive figure by the client, who remits it to FIRS on your behalf and gives you a credit note.

Invoice vs. receipt vs. quotation — what's the difference?

These three get mixed up constantly, especially by first-time business owners:

  • A quotation is sent before any work happens — it's your proposed price, usually with a validity window ("valid for 14 days"). See our quotation generator.
  • An invoice is sent after work is agreed (or delivered) and payment is owed — it's a request for payment, with a due date.
  • A receipt is issued after payment has actually been received — it's proof the transaction is closed. See our receipt generator and guide.

A simple way to remember it: quote → invoice → receipt, in that order, tracking a sale from proposal to payment to proof.

Invoicing without a registered company

You don't need a CAC-registered business to send a professional invoice. Many freelancers and small traders in Nigeria invoice under a trading name with their personal bank account details, and that's perfectly normal for day-to-day business. What matters more than registration status, especially early on, is consistency — the same business name, the same numbering, the same bank details every time, so clients start to recognize and trust your paperwork. Handling partial payments and deposits

A lot of Nigerian small businesses — tailors, event vendors, contractors — work on a deposit-then-balance model, and this is where invoices get messy if you don't structure them properly. The cleanest approach: issue one invoice for the full amount, and track payments against it as they come in, rather than creating a fresh invoice for every partial payment. The invoice should show the total due, amount paid so far, and the outstanding balance — so at any point, both you and the client can see exactly where things stand without cross-referencing three different documents. If a client pays 50% upfront and the balance on delivery, note the deposit clearly on the invoice itself ("50% deposit received: ₦50,000. Balance due on delivery: ₦50,000") rather than only mentioning it verbally.

Recurring invoices and retainer clients

If you invoice the same client monthly — a retainer, a subscription service, ongoing consulting — resist the temptation to just copy last month's invoice and change the date. Each invoice still needs its own unique, sequential number, even if the amount and client are identical every time. It's a small habit that saves real confusion later, especially if a client's finance team is matching invoices against their own payment records.

Invoicing international or diaspora clients from Nigeria

If you're billing a client abroad — increasingly common for Nigerian freelancers and remote workers — a few things change: you'll usually invoice in the client's currency (or USD) rather than Naira, VAT typically doesn't apply to exported services, and payment terms often need to account for international transfer delays. State the currency explicitly on the invoice to avoid any ambiguity, and if you're converting to Naira for your own records, note the exchange rate and date used. Getting paid faster: what actually moves the needle

  • Shorter payment terms, stated clearly. "Due on receipt" or "net 7" gets paid faster than an invoice with no due date at all — ambiguity is an invitation to deprioritize you.
  • Make payment frictionless. Bank details (or a payment link) directly on the invoice, not in a follow-up message the client has to go find.
  • Follow up before it's overdue, not after. A polite reminder a day or two before the due date reads as organized, not pushy — and it's far more effective than chasing a payment that's already late.
  • Keep a record of who owes you what. Once you're invoicing more than a handful of clients a month, tracking overdue invoices in your head stops working — this is exactly what a proper debt dashboard is for.

Common invoicing mistakes that slow down payment

  • No due date — clients pay when it's convenient for them, not for you.
  • Vague line items that make a client question what they're actually paying for.
  • Bank details missing or buried in a separate message.
  • VAT and WHT applied incorrectly, or in the wrong order — see the guide above.
  • No consistent invoice numbering, making disputes harder to resolve.
  • Reusing an invoice template with the wrong client's name still on it — always double-check before sending.

Frequently asked questions

Is a WhatsApp-sent invoice legally valid in Nigeria?
Yes — there's no legal requirement for an invoice to be posted or emailed specifically. What matters is that it's a clear, complete record of the transaction. A PDF invoice shared over WhatsApp is standard practice for Nigerian SMEs and freelancers.

Do I need a registered business to issue invoices?
No. You can invoice under a trading name using your personal bank details. Registration becomes more relevant once you're dealing with larger corporate clients who require it for their own compliance, or once you want the legal protections registration provides.

How many days should I give clients to pay?
Net 7 or net 14 is standard for most small business invoicing in Nigeria. Net 30 is common for larger corporate clients with formal accounts-payable processes, but tends to slow down cash flow for smaller vendors — shorter terms, clearly stated, generally get you paid sooner.

What if a client disputes an invoice?
This is exactly why sequential invoice numbers and itemized descriptions matter — they give you a clear, specific record to point to rather than a vague "you said you'd pay me" conversation.

Can I edit an invoice after sending it?
You can, but it's best practice to treat a sent invoice as final once the client has it — if something needs to change, issue a corrected invoice referencing the original number, rather than silently editing and resending the same one.
