How to Track Business Expenses in Nigeria (Without Losing Your Mind)

August 2026 · 6 min read

Ask most small business owners how much they spent last month and you'll get a rough estimate, not a real number. That gap between "roughly" and "exactly" is where profit quietly disappears — not to theft or bad luck, usually, but to a dozen small, untracked expenses that never got written down anywhere.

Key takeaways
  • Untracked expenses hide your real profit margin — a business can feel busy and still be barely breaking even, or losing money, without expense tracking to show it.
  • Every expense needs four things: date, amount, category, and a receipt — even a phone photo of one.
  • Small recurring expenses (data, transport, small tools) add up more than most owners expect over a year.
  • Categorizing expenses (not just totaling them) is what turns raw numbers into decisions you can actually act on.

Why "I have a rough idea" isn't good enough

Revenue is usually easy to track — money coming in tends to get noticed. Expenses are where things fall apart, because they're spread across dozens of small transactions: transport to deliver an order, data to respond to customer messages, a quick supply run, a subscription renewed automatically. None of these feel significant individually. Added up over a month, they often are.

Without tracking them, a business owner is essentially running blind on the one number that actually determines whether the business is profitable: revenue minus expenses. It's entirely possible to be busy, have steady sales, and still be losing money — and not find out until the gap has grown large enough to be a real problem.

What counts as a business expense

Broadly, anything spent to keep the business running or generate revenue:

  • Inventory and stock — what you buy to sell or use in production.
  • Rent and utilities — shop, office, or workspace costs, including electricity and internet.
  • Transport and logistics — delivery costs, fuel, dispatch riders.
  • Staff costs — salaries, wages, casual labour.
  • Marketing — ads, promotions, printing.
  • Tools and subscriptions — software, POS terminal fees, any recurring service the business depends on.

The common thread: if the business wouldn't have spent it without existing, it's a business expense — even the small, easy-to-forget ones.

The four things every expense record needs. Date, amount, category, and a receipt (a phone photo is enough). Missing any one of these makes the record far less useful later — a total without categories can't tell you where money is going, and an amount without a receipt can't be verified if it's ever questioned.

Categorizing is what makes tracking useful

A single running total of "money spent" answers one question. Categorized expenses answer many more: which category is growing fastest, whether marketing spend is actually producing sales, whether transport costs make sense for the order volume. The difference between tracking and categorizing is the difference between knowing you spent money and knowing what it bought you.

A simple habit that actually sticks

The expense trackers that fail are usually the ones that ask for too much effort per entry. The ones that stick are fast: see a receipt, photograph it, log the amount and category, done — ideally in under thirty seconds. Batching this into one big monthly session almost never works as well as logging in the moment, simply because the details (what was this ₦4,500 transfer actually for?) are much easier to remember immediately than three weeks later.

Frequently asked questions

Should I track personal expenses paid from the business account too?
If a personal expense is ever paid from the business account, it should be logged clearly as a personal draw, not a business expense — mixing the two is exactly what makes bookkeeping unreliable later.

What if I don't have a receipt for something?
Log it anyway with as much detail as you have, and get in the habit of asking for receipts going forward. An imperfect record beats no record — but a receipt is what makes the entry fully defensible if it's ever reviewed.

How often should I review my expenses, not just log them?
Monthly is a reasonable rhythm for most small businesses — frequent enough to catch a spending pattern before it becomes a real problem, infrequent enough not to become a chore in itself.
