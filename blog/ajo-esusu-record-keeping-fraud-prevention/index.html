Why Ajo and Esusu Groups Keep Losing Money to the Person Holding the Pot

August 2026 · 5 min read

Five market women contribute ₦20,000 each, every month, so that each of them gets a ₦100,000 lump sum when it's their turn. It's a system that has worked for generations of traders, artisans, and market women across Nigeria — and it has one structural weak point that's been there the whole time: one person usually holds the cash, and one person usually holds the only record of who's paid. When those two things are true at once, trust is the entire system, and trust is exactly what breaks when the numbers stop adding up.

Key takeaways
  • The most common way Ajo and Esusu groups lose money isn't dishonesty — it's a single point of record-keeping with no way to independently verify it.
  • When contributions are tracked in one person's notebook or memory, small errors and larger problems both go unnoticed until a payout comes up short.
  • Digitizing the record doesn't change how the group runs — the rotation, the trust, and the community structure stay exactly the same.
  • A record-keeping tool is not the same thing as a savings app that custodies the money — worth knowing which one you're actually using.

The failure point isn't usually dishonesty

It's easy to frame Ajo fraud as a story about a bad actor who ran off with the pot. Sometimes that happens. Far more often, the failure is quieter: a collector genuinely trying to do right by the group, tracking contributions in a notebook or a mental tally, and small discrepancies creeping in — a missed entry, a contribution recorded against the wrong week, a member who paid but it never got written down. None of that requires bad intent. It just requires a system where only one person can see the full picture, and everyone else has to take their word for it.

Why this is structural, not personal

The traditional Ajo model concentrates two things in one place: custody of the cash, and the record of who owes what. That concentration is exactly what makes disputes so hard to resolve when they happen — if the only record and the only cash-holder are the same person, there's no independent reference point for anyone else in the group to check against. This isn't a flaw in any individual collector's character. It's a structural gap in how the system has traditionally worked, and it's the same gap every digital Ajo tool on the market today is trying to close.

What actually changes when you digitize the record

The rotation still works the same way. Members still contribute the same amount, on the same schedule, and take their turn in the same order. What changes is who can see the record: instead of one notebook that only the collector controls, every member can check contributions and payout history against a shared, transparent log. Disputes get resolved by pointing at a record everyone already had access to, instead of one person's word against another's memory.

A record-keeping tool is not automatically a custody app

It's worth being precise about what a given tool actually does, because "digital Ajo app" covers two genuinely different things. Some platforms hold the pooled funds themselves, moving money between members through their own rails — that's a real service, but it's a different kind of trust than a traditional Ajo circle asks for. KoboDocs' Ajo & Esusu tracker works the other way: it's a shared ledger, not a custodian. The money moves exactly the way it always has between members — the tool's only job is making sure everyone can see the same record, instead of one person's private notebook being the only version that exists.

Frequently asked questions

Why do Ajo and Esusu groups lose money even when everyone contributes honestly?
The most common failure point isn't dishonest members — it's the record-keeping itself. When one person holds both the cash and the only record of who's paid, there's no independent way to confirm contributions match what's owed, and small discrepancies (or larger ones) go uncaught until a payout comes up short.

Does digitizing an Ajo group mean giving up the traditional system?
No. A digital record doesn't change who contributes, how much, or in what order — it changes who can see and verify the record. The rotation, the trust, and the community structure of Ajo stay exactly the same; only the record-keeping layer changes.

Is a digital Ajo tracker the same as a savings app that holds your money?
Not necessarily — and it's worth checking which one you're using. Some apps custody the pooled funds directly. Others, including KoboDocs' Ajo tool, function purely as a shared, transparent ledger: the money still moves the way it always has between members, and the tool just makes the record everyone can see instead of one person's private notebook.
