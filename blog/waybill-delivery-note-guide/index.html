Waybill vs. Delivery Note: What Nigerian Businesses Actually Need

July 2026 · 6 min read

Goods leave a warehouse, get handed to a dispatch rider, or get loaded onto a truck heading upcountry — and without a document accompanying that movement, there's no clean record of what left, who sent it, who received it, or whether everything arrived in the condition it left in. That's what a waybill or delivery note is for, and the two terms get used almost interchangeably in Nigeria, though they lean toward slightly different situations.

Key takeaways
  • "Waybill" and "delivery note" are used almost interchangeably in Nigerian business practice — both accompany goods in transit and get signed on delivery.
  • A waybill leans toward longer-distance or third-party transport (interstate haulage, logistics companies), while a delivery note is more commonly used for local, direct-to-customer drop-offs.
  • The document should travel with the goods, not be sent separately — its whole purpose is to be checked and signed at the point of handover.
  • A signed delivery note or waybill is your primary evidence if a dispute comes up later about quantity, condition, or whether delivery happened at all.
  • It's not the same as an invoice — a waybill records that goods moved; an invoice records what's owed for them.

Waybill vs. delivery note — is there really a difference?

In day-to-day Nigerian business use, the two terms are largely interchangeable, and most businesses don't lose anything by treating them as the same document. Where a distinction is drawn, "waybill" tends to be used for goods moving over longer distances or through a transport/logistics company (particularly interstate haulage), while "delivery note" is more commonly used for local, direct drop-offs to a customer or branch. Both serve the same core purpose: a paper (or digital) trail confirming what was sent, in what quantity, to whom, and when.

What a waybill or delivery note should include

  • Sender's details — business name, address, and contact.
  • Receiver's details — name, delivery address, and contact.
  • Itemized list of goods — description, quantity, and unit if relevant (cartons, bags, units).
  • Date of dispatch and expected delivery date.
  • Vehicle and driver details — useful for tracking and accountability, especially for third-party transport.
  • Signature lines — for both the person releasing the goods and the person receiving them, confirming quantity and condition on arrival.

Why it shouldn't be skipped, even for "small" deliveries

The value isn't in the paperwork — it's in the signature. A waybill or delivery note only does its job if the receiving party actually signs it at handover, confirming quantity and condition. Skipping this for "just a small delivery" is exactly how disputes over shortages or damage become unresolvable later.

It's tempting to skip a formal document for a quick, informal delivery — but this is precisely the situation where a dispute is hardest to resolve without one. If a customer later claims an item was missing or damaged, a signed delivery note showing what left and what was confirmed received is the clearest evidence either side has.

Waybill vs. invoice — they're not the same thing

A waybill or delivery note documents that goods physically moved from one point to another — it's a logistics record. An invoice documents what's owed for those goods — it's a financial record. Some businesses combine the two into one document for simple transactions, but where goods are delivered before payment is settled (common in B2B and wholesale), keeping them separate makes it clearer to track what's been delivered versus what's been paid for.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a waybill for every delivery, even small local ones?
It's good practice for any delivery where you'd want proof of what was sent and received — for very small, low-value, in-person handoffs some businesses skip it, but anything with meaningful value or distance is worth documenting.

Can a waybill double as proof of payment?
No — it confirms that goods moved and were received, not that payment was made. Use an invoice or receipt for payment records.

Who should keep the signed copy?
Both parties ideally keep a signed copy — the sender for their delivery records, and the receiver as proof of what they confirmed receiving.

What if the receiver refuses to sign?
This is worth noting directly on the document along with the reason given, since it's relevant if a dispute comes up later about whether delivery was properly completed.

Is a digital (WhatsApp-sent) delivery note as valid as a printed, signed one?
A physically signed copy at the point of handover remains the strongest form of proof. A digital version is useful for record-keeping and sharing, but where possible, get an actual signature (photographed or scanned) at delivery rather than relying on a digital copy alone.
