How to Write a Will in Nigeria (and What Happens If You Don't)

August 2026 · 9 min read

Most Nigerians without a Will assume it's something for later — when they're older, richer, or have "something worth leaving." In reality, dying without one (called dying "intestate") hands the decision of who gets what to a fixed legal formula that often doesn't match what the person would have actually wanted, and it can leave a spouse or children in a drawn-out, expensive process to access what should already be theirs.

Here's what a Will actually needs to be valid in Nigeria, what happens if you skip it, and how to get started properly.

Key takeaways
  • Without a Will, your estate is distributed under intestate succession rules (or customary/Sharia law, depending on your state and background) — not necessarily how you'd choose.
  • A valid Will in Nigeria must be in writing, signed by the testator, and witnessed by at least two people who aren't beneficiaries.
  • An executor is the person legally responsible for carrying out your Will — choose someone you trust who's capable of handling the administrative work.
  • If you have children under 18, naming a guardian in your Will is one of the most important — and most often skipped — sections.
  • A Will only takes effect after death; if you want someone to manage your affairs while you're alive, that's a Power of Attorney instead.

What happens if you die without a Will

Dying intestate means your estate is distributed according to a fixed legal formula rather than your own wishes. Depending on your state, background, and the applicable law (the Administration of Estates law in your state, customary law, or Islamic law), this can mean assets go to relatives you wouldn't have chosen, in proportions you wouldn't have picked, and — critically — it often takes far longer and costs more for your family to actually access the estate, since a court-appointed administrator has to be formally established first (unlike an executor you've already named yourself).

This is the single biggest practical reason to have a Will: it isn't really about controlling wealth from beyond the grave, it's about sparing the people you leave behind a slow, sometimes contentious legal process during an already difficult time.

What makes a Will valid in Nigeria

Under the Wills Act (and corresponding state Wills Laws), a valid Will needs to meet a few clear requirements:

  • In writing — a verbal statement of your wishes isn't legally a Will, no matter how many people heard it.
  • Signed by the testator (the person making the Will), or by someone else in their presence and at their direction, if they're physically unable to sign.
  • Witnessed by at least two people, present at the same time, who then also sign the Will themselves.
  • Witnesses should not be beneficiaries — if a witness is also named to inherit something, that gift to them can be invalidated, even though the rest of the Will stays valid.
  • The testator must be of sound mind and generally at least 18 years old (with some exceptions, such as members of the armed forces).

What to actually include

  • Executor(s) — the person or people responsible for carrying out the Will's instructions. Choose someone organized and trustworthy; you can name more than one.
  • Guardian for minor children — if you have children under 18, this is arguably the most important section in the whole document, and it's the one people skip most often because it feels uncomfortable to think about. Without a named guardian, the choice may end up in a court's hands.
  • Specific bequests — particular items or amounts left to particular people (a piece of property, a specific sum, a family item).
  • Residuary clause — what happens to everything else, after specific bequests are handled. Skipping this is a common mistake that leaves part of the estate effectively intestate anyway.
  • Revocation clause — a statement that this Will revokes any previous Wills, to avoid confusion if an older version exists somewhere.
A Will is not the same as a next of kin declaration. Naming someone as your next of kin on a bank or employment form is for contact and emergency purposes only — it does not determine inheritance. Updating a Will

Circumstances change — marriage, divorce, a new child, a new asset, a falling-out with a previously-named beneficiary. A Will isn't a one-time document; it's worth revisiting after any major life event. You can update it either by making a new Will (which should explicitly revoke the old one) or by a formal codicil — a supplementary document that amends specific parts. For most people, especially with something as accessible as a template-based Will, simply making a fresh one when something significant changes is simpler and less error-prone than managing multiple codicils.

Will vs. Power of Attorney — don't confuse the two

This mix-up comes up constantly. A Will only takes effect after you die, and it governs your estate. A Power of Attorney only works while you're alive, authorizing someone to act on your behalf, and it becomes void the moment you die. If you want both — someone to help manage things now, and clear instructions for after — you need both documents, not one covering both purposes.

Common mistakes that cause problems later

  • No residuary clause — leaving assets you didn't specifically mention effectively intestate.
  • Using a beneficiary as a witness — invalidates that person's gift, even if the rest of the Will stands.
  • No named guardian for minor children, leaving that decision to a court.
  • Outdated Wills that don't reflect a divorce, remarriage, or new children.
  • Verbal promises treated as if they carry the same weight as a written, witnessed Will — they don't.
  • Storing the only copy somewhere no one else knows about — a Will that can't be found is functionally the same as not having one. Tell your executor where it is.

Frequently asked questions

What happens to my property in Nigeria if I die without a Will?
Your estate is distributed under intestate succession rules — which vary by state, customary law, or Islamic law depending on your background — rather than your own choices, and the process for your family to access the estate is typically slower and more complex than if you'd named an executor yourself.

Do I need a lawyer to write a valid Will in Nigeria?
Not strictly — a Will is valid as long as it's in writing, signed by you, and witnessed by two people who aren't beneficiaries. For a complex estate (multiple properties, business ownership, blended families), professional legal advice is still worth getting.

Can a beneficiary of my Will also be a witness?
It's best avoided. If a witness is also named as a beneficiary, that specific gift to them can be invalidated, even though the rest of the Will remains valid.

What's the difference between a Will and a Power of Attorney?
A Will only takes effect after death and governs your estate. A Power of Attorney only works while you're alive, authorizing someone to act on your behalf, and becomes void the moment you die — they're not substitutes for each other.

How often should I update my Will?
Whenever a major life event happens — marriage, divorce, a new child, acquiring a significant new asset, or a change in who you'd want as executor or guardian. There's no fixed schedule, but it's worth reviewing after anything that would change your original decisions.
