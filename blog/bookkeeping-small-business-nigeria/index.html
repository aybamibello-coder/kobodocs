Bookkeeping for Small Business in Nigeria: A Practical Starter Guide

August 2026 · 8 min read

Picture a boutique owner in Surulere who kept three years of "accounting" in a cardboard box under the counter — POS slips, bank alerts, supplier invoices, all crumpled together. When her accountant finally sat down with it, it took two weeks to sort through, cost triple the normal fee, and roughly ₦800,000 in real expenses still couldn't be accounted for. It's a more common story than most business owners would like to admit — and it's almost entirely avoidable.

Key takeaways
  • FIRS doesn't expect elaborate accounting systems — just evidence of income and expenses, kept safely, for long enough to be verified.
  • The standard retention period is six years from the end of the relevant tax year, for invoices, receipts, bank statements, payroll records, and filed returns.
  • Small businesses with turnover under ₦100 million and assets under ₦250 million now qualify for a 0% corporate tax rate under recent reforms — but only if your records can actually prove your numbers.
  • A spreadsheet can be enough for a very small business, as long as it's consistent, reconciled regularly, and backed by real documents.

What FIRS actually expects

The requirements are simpler than most business owners assume. There's no obligation to run an elaborate accounting system or hire a full-time accountant from day one. What matters is being able to show, for any given period, what came in, what went out, and proof for both. In practice that means:

  • A record of every sale — even a simple notebook entry with date, description, amount, and payment method counts.
  • Receipts or invoices for every expense.
  • Bank statements that match what your records say.
  • Payroll records, if you have staff.

Inadequate record-keeping is one of the most common issues tax auditors run into with small businesses — not fraud, just incomplete or disorganised records that make it impossible to verify what actually happened.

The six-year rule

Keep tax-relevant records for six years from the end of the relevant tax year. This covers sales invoices, purchase receipts, bank statements, payroll records, and filed returns. For equipment or property, keep the purchase documents for the life of the asset plus six years on top. If fraud or deliberate evasion is ever suspected, there's no time limit on how far back an audit can go — which is one more reason not to treat old records as safe to discard early.

Why this matters more than it used to

Under recent tax reforms, small businesses with annual turnover below ₦100 million and assets not exceeding ₦250 million qualify for a 0% corporate tax rate. That's a genuinely significant benefit — but claiming it (and defending it if ever questioned) depends entirely on having records clean enough to prove your actual numbers. A business with real earnings but chaotic bookkeeping can end up worse off than one with modest earnings and impeccable records, simply because the second business can prove what it's owed.

Five mistakes that get small businesses in trouble

  • Mixing personal and business money. The single most common issue. Without a dedicated business account, every transaction has to be manually untangled from personal spending later — often after it's too late to remember which was which.
  • Paper-only records. Physical receipts get lost, faded, or damaged. A digital record — even a basic one — survives a spilled drink or a house move.
  • Not tracking inventory. If you sell physical goods without tracking what comes in and goes out, pricing and reordering both become guesswork.
  • Waiting until tax season. Bookkeeping done as a once-a-year scramble is where the real errors creep in. It works far better as a habit than an event.
  • No regular reconciliation. Reconciliation just means checking that your records match your actual bank statements. Skipping this is how small errors — or worse, fraud — go unnoticed for months.

A simple system to start today

If bookkeeping feels overwhelming, it's worth starting with just the non-negotiables rather than trying to build a complete system on day one:

  • Open a dedicated business account. Every business Naira goes in and out of one place — this single change does more for financial clarity than almost anything else on this list.
  • Record every sale, consistently. An invoice or receipt for each one, not just a mental note.
  • Keep evidence of every expense. A photo of a receipt is enough — the point is that it exists and is findable later.
  • Reconcile monthly, not yearly. Match your records against your bank statement once a month. It takes minutes when done regularly, and hours (or is simply skipped) when left until year-end.
  • Keep it in one digital place. Scattered WhatsApp screenshots, a notebook, and a folder of receipts are three systems, not one — and three systems is how things fall through the cracks.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need an accountant from day one?
Not necessarily. A very small business can maintain its own records early on, provided it's done consistently. An accountant becomes more valuable as transaction volume grows or when it's time to file returns and you want a second set of eyes on the numbers.

What's the difference between bookkeeping and accounting?
Bookkeeping is the day-to-day recording of transactions — invoices, receipts, payments. Accounting is the higher-level analysis and reporting built on top of those records. Good bookkeeping is what makes good accounting possible.

Can I switch from paper to digital records partway through the year?
Yes — there's no requirement to restart your bookkeeping year. Digitise what you can from earlier in the year and keep everything going forward in the new system.
