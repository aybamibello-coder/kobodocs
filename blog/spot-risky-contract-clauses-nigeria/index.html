How to Spot Risky Clauses in a Nigerian Business Contract Before You Sign

August 2026 · 6 min read

Most small business owners in Nigeria don't get a contract reviewed before signing — not because they don't know it's risky, but because a lawyer's review costs more than the deal is worth, and reading twelve pages of clauses yourself doesn't tell you which ones actually matter. The dangerous clauses are rarely the obvious ones. They're the paragraph that sounds like standard boilerplate but quietly shifts all the risk onto you.

Key takeaways
  • The clauses that hurt most are usually written to sound routine, not alarming.
  • Indemnity, termination, and payment-timing clauses are where one-sided terms hide most often.
  • A contract can reference land, employment, or company law without you noticing it's doing so.
  • You don't need a lawyer for every contract — you need to know which ones are worth paying a lawyer for.

Indemnity clauses: who actually carries the risk

An indemnity clause decides who pays if something goes wrong — a client injured on-site, a defective product, a missed deadline that costs the other party money. A fair clause splits that risk according to who's actually at fault. A one-sided one makes you responsible for practically everything, including problems the other party caused, and it's usually written in dense legal language specifically so it doesn't stand out on a first read.

Termination clauses: how easily can they walk away

Look for how much notice either side has to give to end the contract, and whether the terms are actually equal. It's common to see a contract that lets the client terminate "for convenience" with 24 hours' notice, while you're locked in for 30 or 60 days with penalties for early exit. That asymmetry is often buried in a clause that just says "either party may terminate this agreement" — technically true, practically one-sided.

Payment timing: net-30 isn't always net-30

Watch for payment clauses tied to conditions outside your control — "payment due 30 days after client's internal approval," for instance, where the approval step has no deadline of its own. On paper you have payment terms; in practice, the clock never starts until they decide it does.

Contracts touch more law than they look like they do. A lease clause might conflict with the Land Use Act, an employment term might not hold up against the Labour Act, and a shareholders' agreement might contradict what CAMA actually requires for company decisions. None of this is obvious from reading the contract on its own — it requires knowing what the underlying law says.

What's actually worth a lawyer's time

Not every contract needs full legal review — a small one-off freelance gig and a multi-year lease carry very different stakes. The useful skill is triage: knowing which clauses in a given contract are routine and which ones need a second, more expert look before you sign. That's a different task from reading the whole document line by line, and it's the part most people skip because they don't know where to start.
