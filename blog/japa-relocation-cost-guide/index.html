The Real Cost of Japa: Relocating From Nigeria, Budgeted Properly

July 2026 · 8 min read

The "japa" conversation online tends to focus on visa routes and success stories — much less on the actual number you need to have ready before any of it becomes real. Between visa fees, proof of funds, flights, and the first few months of actually living somewhere new, the true cost of relocating from Nigeria is almost always higher than the headline visa fee suggests.

Here's how to think through the full cost, in the order it actually hits your account.

Key takeaways
  • The visa application fee is the smallest part of the total cost — biometrics, flights, and a settling-in buffer usually add up to far more.
  • Proof of funds requirements are strict, destination-specific, and non-negotiable — most visa routes have a minimum threshold you must clearly demonstrate, scaled by family size.
  • Cost of living varies enormously by city, not just by country — Toronto and Calgary, or London and Manchester, can differ significantly for the same lifestyle.
  • Budget for the gap between arrival and your first paycheck — this is where relocation budgets most commonly run short.
  • Building the proof-of-funds savings takes most people longer than the visa process itself — start that part early.

The upfront relocation cost

Before you even board a flight, there's a stack of costs beyond the visa application fee itself:

  • Visa/application fees — varies significantly by route and destination.
  • Biometrics appointment fees — often a separate charge from the main application.
  • Medical examination — required for several visa categories.
  • Document costs — translations, certified copies, courier fees for physical document submission.
  • Flights — one of the largest single line items, and prices swing significantly by season and how far ahead you book.
  • Settling-in buffer — the cash you need on hand for your first weeks: temporary accommodation, initial groceries, local transport, and a SIM/phone plan, before you're earning locally.

Proof of funds — the number that's easy to underestimate

This isn't a soft guideline. Proof of funds thresholds for routes like Canada's Express Entry, Canadian study permits, and UK visas are specific, published amounts that scale with family size — falling short by even a small margin can be a hard rejection point, not something to negotiate around.

Most major relocation routes require you to demonstrate access to a minimum amount of funds, held for a minimum period, before you apply — and the required amount increases with the number of family members included in the application. This is frequently the single biggest saving target in the entire relocation process, and building it up realistically takes most people considerably longer than the visa processing time itself. Starting early, with a clear target number, matters more here than almost anywhere else in the process.

Monthly cost of living once you arrive

This is where a lot of relocation budgets go wrong — people budget for getting there, but underestimate what the first several months of actually living somewhere costs before their income stabilizes. Rent, groceries, transport, and basic utilities vary considerably even within the same country: Toronto and Calgary, or London and Manchester, are genuinely different cost environments for the same lifestyle, and treating "Canada" or "the UK" as a single number is a common planning mistake.

The gap between arrival and your first paycheck

Even with a confirmed job offer, there's typically a gap of several weeks between arrival and your first paycheck landing — and in that window you're covering rent, groceries, transport, and setup costs (a phone plan, initial furniture, work clothes) with whatever you brought. This gap is one of the most commonly underbudgeted parts of the entire relocation, since it's easy to focus on "getting the visa" and treat everything after arrival as something that'll sort itself out.

How to budget for this properly

  • Separate the three cost buckets — visa/upfront costs, proof of funds (which you generally get back once demonstrated, not spent), and ongoing cost of living — rather than treating relocation as one lump sum.
  • Build proof of funds savings first, since it typically takes the longest and is the strictest requirement.
  • Budget at least 2–3 months of living costs as a landing buffer, beyond what proof of funds alone requires.
  • Price flights across a range of dates rather than assuming a single fixed cost — this line item swings more than people expect.

Frequently asked questions

Is proof of funds money I have to spend, or just show?
For most routes, it's about demonstrating access to funds meeting the threshold for a specified period — not money you're required to spend as part of the process. The specific rules vary by route, so confirm the exact requirement for your specific application.

Can I combine savings with a spouse or family member for proof of funds?
This depends on the specific visa route and how the application is structured — some routes do account for combined family funds where the application includes dependents. Check the specific requirement for your route rather than assuming.

How far in advance should I start saving for proof of funds?
Given how strict and non-negotiable the thresholds tend to be, starting well before you plan to apply is far safer than trying to hit the number right before a deadline — many people underestimate how long this actually takes.

Does cost of living differ that much within the same country?
Yes, often significantly — rent alone can differ substantially between a major city center and a smaller city in the same country, which is why budgeting by specific city rather than country matters.

What's the biggest budgeting mistake people make with japa planning?
Underbudgeting the gap between arrival and a stable income — many plans account carefully for the visa and flight but treat the first few months abroad as something that will simply work out.
