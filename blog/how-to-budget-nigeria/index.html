How to Budget on a Naira Salary in Nigeria (Without a Spreadsheet)

August 2026 · 7 min read

Most budgeting advice online is written for a different economy — stable prices, predictable monthly costs, salaries that stretch the same way every month. That's not the reality most people are budgeting against right now. Prices shift, income is often irregular even for salaried workers (delayed pay, side income, seasonal work), and a "budget" that assumes otherwise falls apart by week two.

Here's a simpler, more realistic approach.

Key takeaways
  • Start from what actually came in last month, not what you hope will come in this month — irregular income needs a different budgeting approach than a fixed salary.
  • Track spending in broad categories (food, transport, rent, data, family support) rather than tracking every single Naira — the broad picture is what actually changes behavior.
  • Build in a buffer for price changes — a budget set once and never revisited quietly stops matching reality within a few months.
  • Ajo/Esusu and cooperative contributions are a legitimate budget line, not an afterthought — treat them like any other fixed monthly commitment.
  • A simple list of "money in vs. money out" beats an elaborate spreadsheet you'll stop updating after two weeks.

Start with last month's actual numbers, not this month's hopes

The most common budgeting mistake is building a plan around what you expect to earn and spend, rather than what actually happened last month. If your income is irregular — commission, side hustle income, delayed salary payments, seasonal work — budgeting against a hoped-for number sets you up to overspend before the money even arrives. Look at your last one to three months of actual income and expenses first, then build the budget from that real baseline.

Broad categories beat granular tracking

You don't need to log every ₦200 you spend on suya to budget well — that level of detail is exactly what makes people abandon budgeting after two weeks. A handful of broad categories is enough to see the real picture and make real decisions:

  • Rent/housing
  • Food & groceries
  • Transport
  • Data & airtime
  • Family support / remittances — a real and often significant line item for many Nigerian households, worth naming explicitly rather than lumping into "miscellaneous"
  • Savings & Ajo/Esusu contributions
  • Everything else

Treat Ajo/Esusu and cooperative contributions as a fixed line, not an afterthought

For a lot of people, Ajo, Esusu, and cooperative thrift contributions are one of the most consistent "savings" mechanisms they have — but because they're social and recurring rather than a bank standing order, they sometimes don't make it onto a formal budget at all, and end up competing with other spending in the moment instead. Put the contribution amount into the budget as a fixed line, the same as rent, so it's protected rather than left to whatever's remaining at the end of the month. Build in a buffer for price changes

A budget written once in January and never revisited tends to quietly stop reflecting reality by mid-year, particularly for food and transport costs, which move more than most other categories. Rather than trying to predict exact future prices, build in a small buffer — even 5-10% — across variable categories, and actually revisit the budget monthly rather than treating it as a one-time document.

What to do when income is genuinely irregular

If your income varies month to month — freelance work, commission, seasonal business — a fixed budget built around one "typical" month will fail in both directions: it feels too tight in a lean month and too loose in a good one. A more realistic approach:

  • Identify your genuinely fixed costs (rent, key bills) and make sure those are covered first, from whatever the lowest realistic month looks like
  • Treat anything above that baseline as variable — extra savings, discretionary spending — rather than committing it in advance
  • In stronger months, resist the urge to permanently raise your fixed commitments based on one good month

A simple structure that actually gets used

The budgeting method most likely to survive past week two isn't the most detailed one — it's the one simple enough to actually update. A short list of money in, money out by category, and what's left over, checked weekly rather than obsessively tracked daily, beats an elaborate spreadsheet that gets abandoned. Frequently asked questions

How should I budget if my income is irregular in Nigeria?
Identify your genuinely fixed costs and cover those from your lowest realistic month's income first, then treat anything above that baseline as variable — extra savings or discretionary spending — rather than committing it in advance based on hoped-for income.

Should Ajo or Esusu contributions be part of my budget?
Yes — treat the contribution amount as a fixed line item, the same way you'd treat rent, so it's protected rather than competing with other spending decisions at the end of the month.

How detailed should my budget categories be?
Broad categories — rent, food, transport, data, family support, savings — are usually enough to see the real picture and change behavior. Extremely granular tracking of every small purchase is exactly what causes most people to abandon budgeting within a few weeks.

How often should I update my budget?
Monthly, at minimum — a budget set once and never revisited tends to stop matching reality within a few months, particularly as food and transport costs shift.

Do I need a spreadsheet to budget properly?
No — a simple list of money in, money out by broad category, and what's left over is enough for most people, and is far more likely to actually be kept up than an elaborate spreadsheet.
