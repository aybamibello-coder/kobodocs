Freelance Contracts in Nigeria: How to Protect Yourself Before You Start Work

July 2026 · 7 min read

Most freelance disputes in Nigeria don't come from bad clients — they come from an agreement that was never actually written down. A verbal "yes, let's start" over WhatsApp feels efficient in the moment, but it leaves nothing to point back to when the client suddenly wants three more revisions, or payment is two weeks late with no explanation.

A simple written contract fixes almost all of this, and it doesn't need to be complicated to work.

Key takeaways
  • A clear scope of work is the single most important clause — it's what stops "just one more small thing" from turning into unpaid extra work.
  • Payment terms should specify the amount, the schedule (upfront deposit, milestones, or full payment on delivery), and what happens if payment is late.
  • Revision limits protect both sides — clients get clarity on what's included, freelancers avoid unlimited unpaid tweaks.
  • Who owns the final work, and when ownership transfers (usually on full payment), should be stated explicitly rather than assumed.
  • A contract doesn't need to be long to be effective — a clear one-page agreement beats an intimidating twenty-page document neither side actually reads.

Why a WhatsApp agreement isn't enough

A back-and-forth chat can capture the general idea of a project, but it rarely captures the details that actually matter once work starts — exactly what's included, what counts as a revision versus new work, and what happens if the client goes quiet mid-project. A short written contract, signed by both sides before work begins, removes the ambiguity that most freelance disputes actually come down to.

What a freelance contract should include

  • Scope of work — specifically what's being delivered, not a general description of the project. Vague scope is the top cause of freelance disputes.
  • Total fee and payment schedule — the amount, and whether it's paid upfront, in milestones, or on completion.
  • Timeline — start date and expected delivery date, with any dependency on the client providing materials or feedback on time.
  • Revision policy — how many rounds of revisions are included before additional work is billed separately.
  • Ownership and usage rights — when the client gains full rights to the delivered work (typically on full payment, not on delivery).
  • Late payment terms — what happens if payment is delayed past the agreed schedule.
  • Termination clause — what happens if either side needs to end the project early, and what's owed for work already done.

Scope creep — and how to actually prevent it

"Can you just also add..." is one of the most familiar phrases in freelance work, and it's exactly what a clear scope-of-work clause is meant to prevent. When the contract specifically states what's included, anything beyond that is visibly additional work — which makes it far easier to say "that's outside the current scope, here's what it would cost to add" instead of either doing it for free or having an awkward conversation with nothing to point back to.

Handling late or missing payment

State it upfront, not after the fact: A contract that specifies a late payment fee, a pause-on-delivery clause, or a required deposit before work begins gives you something concrete to reference — rather than trying to negotiate consequences after the payment is already overdue.

Common approaches include requiring a partial deposit before work starts (protecting against a client who disappears entirely), tying final delivery to full payment, and stating a specific late fee or grace period. Whichever approach you use, it works far better when it's written into the agreement both sides signed, rather than raised for the first time when payment is already late.

Who owns the work, and when

It's worth being explicit that full ownership and usage rights transfer to the client on full payment — not on delivery. This protects the freelancer if a client takes delivery of the work but then delays or refuses final payment; without this clause, ownership can be ambiguous exactly when it matters most.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a lawyer to draft a freelance contract?
For most standard freelance work, no — a clear, complete written agreement covering scope, payment, and ownership is generally sufficient. Complex or high-value engagements are where legal review starts to be worth the cost.

Is a contract still valid if it's signed digitally or just agreed over email?
Yes — a written agreement doesn't need a wet-ink signature to be binding. A clearly agreed-to written contract, even over email, is far stronger evidence than an informal chat exchange.

What if the client refuses to sign a contract?
This is generally a signal worth paying attention to — a legitimate client with nothing to hide typically has no issue agreeing to clear terms in writing before work starts.

How do I handle a client who keeps requesting "small" changes outside the agreed scope?
Point back to the scope-of-work clause and offer the additional work as a separately billed add-on — this is exactly the situation a clear scope clause is designed to resolve cleanly.

Should the contract specify a currency and payment method?
Yes, especially for freelancers working with clients outside Nigeria — stating the currency and accepted payment method upfront avoids disputes over exchange rates or unexpected transfer fees later.
