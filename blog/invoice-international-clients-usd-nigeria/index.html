How Nigerian Freelancers Should Invoice International Clients

August 2026 · 6 min read

A growing number of Nigerian freelancers earn primarily from international clients — a US company paying for design work, a UK agency paying for writing, a European startup paying for development. The work itself isn't that different from working with a local client. The invoicing side has a few extra things worth getting right, mostly around currency and clarity.

Key takeaways
  • Bill in the client's currency (usually USD or GBP) — it's what they've budgeted in and expect to see.
  • The invoiced amount is fixed; what changes with exchange rates is how much it converts to in naira, not the invoice itself.
  • State the payment platform clearly (Wise, Payoneer, bank wire) so there's no back-and-forth about how to pay.
  • Keep your own naira-denominated records too — for your own bookkeeping, separate from what the client sees.

Which currency to invoice in

For an international client, invoicing in their own currency is almost always the right call. A US client paying a Nigerian freelancer generally expects a dollar figure — it matches how they budgeted the project, and it avoids putting the burden of currency conversion on them. Invoicing in naira to a client who's never handled naira before just adds friction to getting paid.

The exchange rate question

A common point of confusion: does the invoice amount change if the exchange rate moves between when it's sent and when it's paid? No — the invoiced amount is fixed in whatever currency you billed (say, $500). What changes is purely how much that $500 becomes in naira once it's converted, which depends on the exchange rate and the platform used at the time of conversion. The invoice itself doesn't need to account for exchange rate movement; only your own mental math about take-home naira does.

Where the money actually goes matters as much as the invoice. How you receive and convert international payments (Payoneer, Wise, a domiciliary account, a fintech platform) affects fees and the effective rate you get far more than anything on the invoice itself. It's worth understanding your platform's fee structure separately from getting the invoice right — they're two different problems.

What to include that a purely local invoice might not need

  • Currency stated explicitly — "$500 USD," not just "500," removes any ambiguity.
  • Payment method or platform — telling the client upfront whether you're expecting payment via Wise, Payoneer, PayPal, or bank wire avoids a delay while they ask.
  • Clear itemization — international clients, especially agencies and companies with their own accounting processes, often need a clear breakdown for their own internal records, not just a lump sum.

Keeping your own records straight

Even when invoicing a client in dollars, it's worth keeping your own bookkeeping in naira (or tracking both) for your own financial picture and any tax obligations that apply to your income. The client-facing invoice and your internal record don't have to be the same document — the client sees the dollar invoice; your own books can reflect what actually landed in your account after conversion and fees.

Frequently asked questions

Should I include my Nigerian bank account details on an international invoice?
Generally no, unless the client specifically pays via direct bank wire — most international freelance payments route through a platform (Payoneer, Wise) rather than a direct SWIFT transfer, so the platform's payment details matter more than your local bank account on the invoice itself.

Do I still need to show my business name and details on an international invoice?
Yes — the same professionalism and clarity that applies to local invoices applies here. Your name or business name, contact details, and a clear description of the work still matter for an international client's own records.

What if the client asks to pay in naira instead?
Some clients with an existing Nigerian presence or team may prefer this — if so, it's worth agreeing on the exchange rate or naira figure explicitly upfront rather than leaving it ambiguous on the invoice.
