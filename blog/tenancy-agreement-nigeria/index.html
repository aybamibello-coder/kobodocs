Tenancy Agreement in Nigeria: What Every Landlord and Tenant Needs to Know

July 2026 · 10 min read

Whether you're a landlord letting out a flat in Lekki or a tenant about to hand over a year's rent in one lump sum — which, let's be honest, is how renting works in most of Nigeria — a proper tenancy agreement is the one document that protects both sides when memory gets fuzzy or a dispute comes up eight months in.

This guide covers what a Nigerian tenancy agreement should actually contain, how notice to quit works, the difference between agency fees and caution fees (a constant source of confusion), and how to put a solid agreement together without hiring a lawyer for a straightforward residential let.

Key takeaways
  • A tenancy agreement should always be in writing, even though verbal agreements are technically enforceable in Nigeria — writing is what actually protects you in a dispute.
  • Notice to quit periods are set by the length of the tenancy, not by landlord preference: 1 week for weekly tenancies, 1 month for monthly, 3 months for quarterly, and 6 months for yearly tenancies.
  • Rent, agency fee, and caution fee are three separate costs — agency fee (commonly ~10% of annual rent) goes to the agent, caution fee is a refundable deposit held against damage, and neither should be confused with rent itself.
  • Lagos State has its own Tenancy Law (2011) governing notice periods and eviction procedure — other states largely follow similar conventions but check local practice.

What a tenancy agreement actually needs to include

At minimum, a proper Nigerian tenancy agreement should cover:

  • Full names and addresses of both landlord and tenant.
  • A clear description of the property — address, unit, and what's included (fittings, parking, generator access, etc.).
  • The tenancy term — start date and whether it's a yearly, monthly, or fixed-term tenancy.
  • Rent amount and payment schedule — most Nigerian residential tenancies are paid annually upfront, but state it explicitly rather than assuming.
  • Caution fee (security deposit) — the amount, and the conditions under which it's refundable at the end of the tenancy.
  • Responsibilities for repairs and maintenance — who fixes what, and who pays for it.
  • Rules on subletting — most agreements explicitly prohibit the tenant from subletting without the landlord's written consent.
  • Notice period for either party to end the tenancy.
  • Signatures of both parties, ideally with a witness for each side.

Rent, agency fee, and caution fee — three different costs

This is where a lot of first-time tenants in Nigeria get caught off guard: the amount you need to move in is almost never just the rent. It's commonly rent plus an agency fee plus a caution fee, and each one serves a completely different purpose:

  • Rent is what you're paying to occupy the property, usually for a full year upfront.
  • Agency fee (or agent's commission) goes to the real estate agent who arranged the letting — commonly around 10% of the annual rent, though this varies by agent and location. It's a one-time fee, not something you pay every year.
  • Caution fee (security deposit) is held by the landlord against damage to the property or unpaid utility bills, and should be refundable at the end of the tenancy if the property is handed back in good condition. This is separate from rent and shouldn't be used to cover the final year's rent unless both parties agree to that in writing.
Get it in writing: Whatever the caution fee amount is, and whatever conditions apply to getting it back, put it directly in the tenancy agreement. "We agreed verbally" is the single most common tenancy dispute in Nigeria, and it's entirely avoidable.

Notice to quit: how much notice is legally required

One of the most searched — and most misunderstood — parts of Nigerian tenancy law is how much notice a landlord must give before a tenant is required to vacate. The notice period is tied to the tenancy type, not to landlord preference, and generally follows this convention across most Nigerian states:

  • Weekly tenancy: 1 week's notice
  • Monthly tenancy: 1 month's notice
  • Quarterly tenancy: 3 months' notice (one quarter)
  • Yearly tenancy: 6 months' notice

After the notice to quit expires, if the tenant still hasn't left, the landlord must typically also serve a "7 days' notice of owner's intention to apply to recover premises" before going to court — a landlord cannot lawfully lock a tenant out or forcibly remove their belongings without following this process, no matter how overdue the rent is.

Does a tenancy agreement need to be registered or stamped?

Tenancy and lease agreements in Nigeria are technically subject to stamp duty, payable to the relevant state or federal tax authority, and an unstamped agreement can face restrictions if it's ever tendered as evidence in court. In practice, a large share of residential tenancy agreements in Nigeria are signed and used without formal stamping, especially for shorter, lower-value tenancies — but if the rent is substantial or the tenancy is long-term, getting the agreement stamped is worth the modest cost for the added legal certainty. Where required, this is done through FIRS or your state's internal revenue service.

Lagos Tenancy Law vs. other states

Lagos State has its own dedicated Tenancy Law (2011), which formally sets out notice periods, eviction procedure, and landlord/tenant obligations for tenancies within Lagos. Other states generally follow similar conventions in practice, often under older recovery-of-premises legislation, but specific notice periods and court procedures can vary. If you're renting or letting outside Lagos, it's worth confirming local practice — particularly around notice periods and where a landlord-tenant dispute would actually be heard.

Are verbal tenancy agreements valid?

Yes — a verbal tenancy agreement can be legally enforceable in Nigeria, but it's a poor idea to rely on one. Without anything in writing, a dispute over the rent amount, the caution fee terms, or what was actually agreed on repairs becomes a matter of one person's word against another's. A simple written agreement, even a short one, removes almost all of that risk for both landlord and tenant.

What happens if the property changes hands mid-tenancy?

If a landlord sells the property during an active tenancy, the tenancy agreement generally remains binding on the new owner for its remaining term — a change in ownership doesn't automatically void a tenant's right to occupy. That said, this is exactly the kind of situation where having a clear, written, signed agreement (rather than a verbal understanding with the original landlord) makes the difference between a smooth handover and a genuine dispute.

Common tenancy disputes and how to avoid them

  • Disagreement over caution fee refund — avoided by stating the refund conditions clearly in the agreement upfront, not negotiating them at move-out.
  • Confusion over who pays for repairs — avoided by explicitly assigning responsibility for structural repairs (landlord) vs. day-to-day maintenance (tenant) in the agreement.
  • Rent increases without proper notice — a landlord generally can't increase rent mid-tenancy; increases apply at renewal, and should be communicated with reasonable notice before the renewal date.
  • Subletting without consent — avoided by an explicit subletting clause, since this is one of the most common causes of landlord-tenant breakdown.
  • No agreement in writing at all — the root cause of most of the disputes above, and the easiest one to fix.

Frequently asked questions

Can a landlord evict a tenant without going to court in Nigeria?
No. Even after a valid notice to quit has expired, a landlord must generally follow the proper legal recovery-of-premises process — including further notice — rather than forcibly removing a tenant or their belongings. Self-help eviction can expose the landlord to legal liability.

Is the caution fee the same as the last month's or last year's rent?
No — they're different things. The caution fee is a refundable security deposit against damage, while rent is payment for occupancy. Using one to cover the other should only happen if both parties explicitly agree to it in writing.

Do I need a lawyer to draft a tenancy agreement?
For a standard residential tenancy, no — a clear, complete written agreement covering the essentials is generally sufficient. For high-value commercial leases or unusual terms, having a lawyer review the agreement is worth the cost.

Can rent be paid monthly instead of yearly upfront?
Yes, though annual upfront payment remains the norm for residential tenancies in much of Nigeria. Monthly or quarterly payment terms are increasingly offered by some landlords and platforms — whatever is agreed should be stated explicitly in the tenancy agreement, since "annual" is otherwise assumed by default.

What if the tenancy agreement doesn't specify a notice period?
If the agreement is silent, the statutory notice period tied to the tenancy type (weekly, monthly, quarterly, yearly) generally applies by default. It's still far better practice to state the notice period explicitly, so there's no room for dispute over which default applies.
