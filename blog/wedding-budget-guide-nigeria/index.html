How to Budget a Nigerian Wedding Without Losing Control of the Costs

August 2026 · 8 min read

Nigerian weddings have a well-earned reputation for scale — sometimes two full ceremonies (traditional and white wedding), each with its own guest list, vendors, and expectations. Without a clear budget set early, costs have a way of drifting upward one "small addition" at a time until the final number is nothing like what anyone originally planned for.

Key takeaways
  • Guest count is the single biggest lever on total cost — it multiplies catering, aso-ebi, venue size, and seating all at once.
  • City matters enormously — the same wedding can cost significantly more in Lagos than in a smaller city, largely driven by venue and vendor rates.
  • Aso-ebi can offset costs when managed as an actual line item with tracked contributions, but becomes its own source of overspend when treated informally.
  • Traditional and white weddings are often budgeted as one event when they're really two — each with its own guest list, vendors, and costs.
  • Setting a hard total cap before booking any vendor is what actually prevents budget drift — deciding category by category as you go rarely holds the line.

The major cost categories

  • Venue — usually one of the largest single costs, and highly dependent on city and date.
  • Catering — scales directly with guest count, making this the category most affected by an expanding guest list.
  • Aso-ebi and attire — fabric, tailoring, and coordination costs, for the couple and often for family and close friends.
  • Photography and videography — increasingly a significant line item as expectations for coverage quality have risen.
  • Music and entertainment — MC, band or DJ, and often live performance costs for larger events.
  • Décor — venue styling, backdrop, and table setup, which can vary enormously in cost depending on scale.
  • Traditional wedding costs — often budgeted separately from the white wedding, since it frequently has its own guest list and requirements (see our traditional wedding list guide for what that typically includes).

Why guest count is the real budget lever

It's tempting to think of guest count as a fixed constraint and focus on trimming individual vendor costs instead — but guest count itself is what multiplies almost every other cost category. Catering, aso-ebi coordination, seating, and even venue size scale directly with headcount. Cutting the guest list by even a modest percentage often moves the total budget more than negotiating hard on any single vendor.

Why city changes the number so much

The same wedding — same scale, same style — can cost meaningfully more in Lagos than in a smaller city, largely driven by venue rates and vendor pricing rather than anything about the wedding itself. This is worth factoring in early, especially for couples deciding between hosting in a bigger city for convenience versus a smaller one for cost.

Aso-ebi — cost center or cost offset?

Track it like an actual budget line, not an afterthought. Aso-ebi contributions can meaningfully offset overall costs when tracked properly — who's paid, how much, and for what. Left informal, it becomes just another expense with no return, and a common source of awkward follow-ups after the event.

Aso-ebi is often one of the more financially significant parts of a Nigerian wedding, and how it's handled makes a real difference to the overall budget. Tracked properly — with clear contributions recorded against who's paid and what they're receiving — it can meaningfully offset other costs. Handled informally, it tends to become just another cost with no real return, and often a source of confusion or awkward chasing after the event.

Treat traditional and white weddings as two budgets

Many couples plan the traditional wedding and the white wedding as one combined budget, when in practice they're often two separate events with separate guest lists, separate vendors, and separate cost structures. Budgeting them individually — rather than one combined number — makes it much easier to see where the money is actually going and to catch overspend in one event before it eats into the other.

How to actually hold the line on cost

  • Set a hard total cap before booking anything — deciding budget category by category as planning progresses is how most weddings drift over budget.
  • Finalize the guest list early, since almost every other cost depends on it.
  • Get itemized quotes from vendors, not lump-sum packages, so you can see exactly where cost additions are coming from.
  • Track aso-ebi as an actual budget line with contributions recorded, not an informal side arrangement.
  • Build in a contingency buffer — most weddings have at least a few unplanned additions, and budgeting for that upfront avoids scrambling later.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage of the budget typically goes to catering?
This varies by event, but catering is consistently one of the largest categories since it scales directly with guest count — it's worth budgeting it as a priority line rather than an afterthought.

Should aso-ebi contributions be treated as income for budgeting purposes?
It's reasonable to budget it as an offsetting contribution to specific costs (fabric, coordination) rather than general income — tracking it against what it's specifically funding keeps the budget clearer.

Is it cheaper to combine the traditional and white wedding into one event?
It can reduce some costs (a shared venue booking, for instance), but often means a larger combined guest list and can complicate rather than simplify the budget — this depends heavily on the specific families and preferences involved.

How much should we set aside as a contingency buffer?
There's no universal figure, but building in some buffer for unplanned additions is far better than assuming the initial budget will hold exactly as planned — most weddings have at least a few unexpected costs.

What's the most common wedding budgeting mistake in Nigeria?
Letting the guest list grow after the initial budget is set — since guest count drives so many other costs, an expanding list is one of the most common ways a wedding budget quietly grows beyond the original plan.
