How to Price Your Products or Services in Nigeria

August 2026 · 7 min read

Two mistakes account for most bad pricing decisions in Nigerian small businesses: copying a competitor's price without knowing their actual costs, and setting a price once and never revisiting it while everything around it — ingredient costs, transport, rent — keeps moving. Both lead to the same place: working hard and ending the month with less profit than the effort deserved.

Key takeaways
  • Start from your actual costs — materials, time, overhead — not from what you think customers will accept or what a competitor charges.
  • Your own time has a cost, even if you're not paying yourself a formal salary — leaving it out of the calculation is one of the most common pricing mistakes.
  • Copying a competitor's price only works if you know their cost structure is similar to yours — often it isn't.
  • Prices need periodic revisiting, not just at launch — cost inflation quietly erodes margins on a price set once and forgotten.
  • Being the cheapest option isn't a strategy by itself — it works only if your costs genuinely allow it, not because it feels safer.

Start from cost, not from a guess

The most reliable starting point for any price is your actual cost to deliver — materials, direct labor or your own time, and a reasonable share of overhead (rent, data, transport, tools). Add your desired margin on top of that real number. This sounds obvious, but it's skipped constantly in favor of "what feels right" or "what others charge," both of which can lead to underpricing without the business owner realizing it until cash flow gets tight.

Your time is a real cost — include it

If you're a service provider — a tailor, a designer, a consultant — your own time is the single most commonly excluded cost in DIY pricing. "I'm not paying myself a salary" doesn't mean your time is free; it means the cost is hidden, showing up later as burnout or as a business that technically makes sales but never actually pays its owner properly. Assign your time a realistic hourly value and build it into every price, even if you don't formally draw that amount as a salary yet.

Why copying a competitor's price is risky

Matching a competitor's price only makes sense if your cost structure is genuinely similar to theirs — same supplier costs, same scale, same overhead. A larger competitor buying materials in bulk, or one operating from a lower-rent location, may have real cost advantages you don't have. Copying their price without knowing this can mean matching a number that works for them but quietly loses you money.

Building in a margin that actually survives inflation

A margin that looks healthy at the moment you set a price can shrink fast if input costs move and the price doesn't move with them. Rather than setting a price once and leaving it, build a habit of reviewing it against current costs every few months — particularly for anything with imported components, fuel-dependent delivery, or materials with volatile pricing.

A quick sanity check: if you haven't revisited a price in six months, it's worth recalculating from current costs rather than assuming the original margin still holds — prices for inputs rarely stay flat for that long.

Pricing for different business types

  • Product-based businesses (retail, food, fashion): cost of goods + your time in preparation/handling + overhead share + margin.
  • Service-based businesses (consulting, freelance work, trades): your hourly rate × time required + materials, if any + margin. Consider whether project-based flat pricing or hourly billing suits the specific job better.
  • Businesses with recurring clients: consider whether a standing/retainer arrangement makes more sense than repeatedly re-quoting the same type of work.

Common pricing mistakes

  • Excluding your own labor from the cost calculation entirely.
  • Setting a price once and never adjusting it as costs shift.
  • Racing to be the cheapest without checking whether your actual costs support that position.
  • Copying competitor pricing without understanding their cost structure.
  • Not accounting for payment terms — if you're extending credit to clients, the cost of that delay (and risk of late payment) belongs somewhere in the pricing conversation too.

Frequently asked questions

What's the biggest mistake small businesses make when pricing in Nigeria?
Leaving their own time out of the cost calculation, and copying a competitor's price without knowing whether that competitor's cost structure is actually similar to their own — both lead to underpricing without the business owner realizing it right away.

Should I price based on what competitors charge?
Only if you understand their cost structure well enough to know a similar price would work for you too — a competitor with bulk-buying power or lower overhead can sustain a price that would lose you money.

How often should I revisit my prices?
Regularly — every few months is a reasonable habit, especially for anything with imported components or volatile input costs. A price that made sense six months ago may no longer reflect current costs.

How do I price my own time as a service provider?
Assign your time a realistic hourly value based on what your skills and effort are actually worth, and build that into every price — even if you're not formally drawing a salary yet, your time still has a real cost.

Is being the cheapest option a good pricing strategy?
Only if your actual costs genuinely support it — being cheapest without the cost structure to back it up erodes margin and isn't sustainable, regardless of how it affects short-term sales volume.
