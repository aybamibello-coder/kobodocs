Ajo, Esusu & Adashe: How Nigeria's Traditional Savings Circles Actually Work

August 2026 · 7 min read

Long before microfinance banks and savings apps, Nigerians were already solving the same problem — how to save disciplined, meaningful amounts and access a lump sum when it's actually needed — through rotating savings circles. Known as ajo or esusu in the south and adashe in the north, the concept is the same everywhere: a group contributes regularly, and each member takes a turn receiving the full pot.

Key takeaways
  • Ajo, esusu, and adashe are regional names for the same core structure — a rotating savings and credit association where members contribute regularly and take turns receiving the full pot.
  • Trust between members is the real collateral — there's typically no formal contract or legal enforcement mechanism behind a traditional circle.
  • The rotation order matters and is usually agreed upfront — by seniority, by need, or by a fair method like a draw.
  • The biggest risks are an organizer absconding with funds or a member defaulting once they've already received their payout.
  • A neutral, transparent contribution tracker reduces disputes significantly, without requiring anyone to hand control of the money to a third party.

How the rotation actually works

A group of members agrees to contribute a fixed amount at a fixed interval — weekly, monthly, or whatever cadence the group sets. Each cycle, one member receives the full pooled amount from everyone else's contribution. This continues until every member has received their turn once, at which point the cycle either ends or restarts. For the person receiving early in the rotation, it functions like an interest-free loan; for someone receiving later, it functions more like a disciplined forced-savings plan.

How the order gets decided

There's no single standard — groups commonly decide the payout order by seniority or relationship within the group, by demonstrated need (someone with an urgent expense goes earlier), or by a neutral method like a draw to keep it fair and avoid disputes about favoritism. Whatever method is used, it's worth agreeing on it clearly at the start, since disagreements over "why did they go before me" are a common source of friction later.

Trust is the actual collateral

There's usually no formal contract behind this. Unlike a bank loan, a traditional ajo/esusu/adashe circle typically runs on social trust and community accountability — not a legally enforceable agreement. This is exactly why membership tends to be limited to people who genuinely know and trust each other.

This is what makes the system work at all — and also its biggest vulnerability. Because there's rarely a formal contract, the entire arrangement depends on social accountability: reputational consequences for defaulting, and the expectation that everyone will eventually need the group's trust again. This is why most circles are formed among people who already know each other well — coworkers, church or mosque members, extended family, or a tight community group — rather than strangers.

The two real risks

  • Organizer risk — where one person collects everyone's contributions before distributing, there's a risk they simply don't (absconding with the pooled funds). This is why some groups avoid a single central collector and instead have members contribute and confirm directly.
  • Member default risk — someone who's already received their payout early in the rotation may stop contributing afterward, leaving the remaining members short. This is one of the more common disputes in informal circles, and part of why the payout order matters — some groups deliberately place less-established members later in the rotation.

Tracking contributions properly reduces disputes

A large share of ajo/esusu disputes come down to disagreement over who's actually paid and who hasn't — memory and informal notes only go so far with a group of any real size. A shared, transparent record that every member can see — who's contributed, who's received their payout, and who's next — removes most of the ambiguity that turns into real conflict later.

Frequently asked questions

Is ajo/esusu legal in Nigeria?
Yes — it's a longstanding informal financial practice, not a regulated financial product. It operates on private agreement between members rather than under formal banking regulation.

What happens if someone defaults after receiving their payout?
This depends entirely on the group's own arrangement, since there's typically no formal legal enforcement mechanism — it's handled through social and community pressure, which is exactly why membership trust matters so much upfront.

Should one person hold all the money, or should members pay each other directly?
Both models exist — a central collector is more common for convenience, but it concentrates risk in one person. Some groups prefer members contributing and confirming directly to reduce that single point of failure.

How is ajo different from a formal savings account?
A formal savings account is individually held and doesn't involve group rotation or reliance on other members — ajo's core value (and risk) is that it's a group arrangement built on mutual trust, giving early recipients effectively interest-free access to a lump sum.
