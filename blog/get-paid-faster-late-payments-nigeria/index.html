How to Get Paid Faster: Managing Late-Paying Clients in Nigeria

August 2026 · 6 min read

Ask almost any small business owner in Nigeria what their biggest operational headache is, and "clients who don't pay on time" comes up fast. It's rarely one dramatic non-payment — it's the slow drip of invoices that go two, three, six weeks past due, each one small enough to let slide, until the cumulative effect shows up as a cash flow problem with no single cause to point at.

Key takeaways
  • Payment terms only work if they're stated clearly on the invoice itself, not assumed or discussed verbally once.
  • The first reminder matters most — sent right at the due date, not weeks after.
  • Specific, short messages referencing the exact invoice and amount outperform vague follow-ups.
  • A pattern of late payment from one client is information — it should change how you work with them, not just how you feel about it.

Most late payment starts with unclear terms

Before chasing a late payment, it's worth checking whether the terms were ever actually stated. "I'll pay you soon" or "let me sort you out" agreed over a phone call isn't a payment term — it's a hope. A due date printed on the invoice itself, agreed before work starts, removes the ambiguity that lets a payment slide indefinitely without ever technically being "late."

This is also why invoicing promptly matters more than it seems. An invoice sent the same day work is delivered starts the clock immediately; one sent two weeks later, almost as an afterthought, quietly signals that timing isn't really being tracked — and clients respond to that signal, consciously or not.

The first reminder is the one that matters most

There's a natural reluctance to chase a client for money — it can feel awkward, especially with someone you have an ongoing relationship with. But the data on this is consistent across most collections practices: the earlier and more specific a reminder is, the more likely it gets paid without escalation. Waiting a month "to be polite" usually just makes the eventual conversation harder, not easier.

"Hi [Name], just a quick note that Invoice #INV-0042 for ₦85,000 was due yesterday. Let me know if there's anything you need from me to process it — happy to resend if it got buried."

Short, specific, and gives them an easy way to respond without feeling accused. Most people who intended to pay and simply forgot will settle it within a day or two of a message like this.

When a reminder isn't enough

If a first reminder doesn't get a response within a few days, the second message can afford to be slightly firmer — still professional, but clearer that the invoice needs attention, and specific about what happens next (a call, a revised deadline, or a note that further work is on hold until the balance is cleared). Escalating too slowly costs money; escalating too aggressively too early can damage a relationship that was otherwise fine. The right pace is usually: gentle at the due date, clearer at one week overdue, and direct at two weeks overdue.

Track the pattern, not just the invoice. One late payment from a normally reliable client is worth a gentle nudge. Three late payments in a row from the same client is worth a different conversation — about revised terms, a deposit requirement, or whether the relationship is worth the cash flow strain it's creating.

When to stop extending credit

It's uncomfortable to think about, but a client who reliably pays late is costing the business something real — not just the delayed cash, but the time spent chasing, the stress, and the opportunity cost of that attention going somewhere else. There's no universal rule for when to cut a client off, but a useful test: if a client's payment pattern would make you hesitate to take on more work from them, that hesitation is information worth acting on — a deposit upfront, shorter payment terms, or in some cases, simply not accepting further work from them.

Frequently asked questions

Should late payment terms include a penalty or interest charge?
Some businesses add a late fee clause to invoices, and it can help — but only if it's stated upfront and actually enforced consistently. An unenforced late fee clause teaches clients it's optional.

Is it worth asking for a deposit upfront?
For larger jobs or new clients without payment history, a partial deposit before work begins is one of the most effective ways to reduce late-payment risk — it filters out clients who were never going to pay reliably before any work is done.

How do I keep track of who owes what without it becoming a full-time job?
This is exactly the kind of repetitive tracking worth automating rather than managing in a notebook or memory — a system that shows overdue invoices at a glance and makes sending a reminder a one-click action removes most of the friction that causes chasing to get postponed.
