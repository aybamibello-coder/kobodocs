How to Write a Price Quotation That Actually Wins the Job

August 2026 · 6 min read

A quotation is often the first real document a potential client sees from your business, and a vague or disorganized one can cost you the job before price even becomes the deciding factor. A clear, complete quotation does more than state a number — it signals that you know exactly what you're doing.

Key takeaways
  • A quotation is a formal price offer before work starts — different from an invoice (which requests payment) or a proforma invoice (a preliminary bill, often for imports).
  • A validity period isn't optional — without one, you're implicitly on the hook for a price that might no longer make sense weeks or months later.
  • A vague scope of work is the single biggest cause of "but I thought this was included" disputes after a quote is accepted.
  • Once a client accepts, converting the quotation into an invoice with matching line items avoids confusion and mismatched figures at payment time.
  • Clear payment terms on the quotation itself — not negotiated after acceptance — set expectations before either side has committed.

Quotation vs. invoice vs. proforma invoice

These three get used loosely, but they serve different purposes:

  • Quotation — a formal price offer, sent before work begins, that the client can accept or negotiate.
  • Invoice — a request for payment, sent after work is delivered (or at agreed milestones), based on what was actually agreed.
  • Proforma invoice — a preliminary bill, often used for imports or advance payment requests, that looks like an invoice but isn't a final demand for payment.

Using the right one at the right stage isn't just semantics — a client reading "invoice" when you meant "quotation" can create real confusion about whether payment is actually due yet.

What a proper quotation should include

  • Itemized scope — specific line items, not a single lump-sum figure, so the client can see exactly what they're paying for.
  • Unit prices and quantities where relevant, not just a final total.
  • A validity period — how long the quoted price stands before it needs to be re-confirmed.
  • Payment terms — deposit required, payment schedule, and accepted payment methods.
  • What's excluded — anything explicitly not covered, to prevent "I assumed this was included" disputes later.
  • A signature or acceptance line — so there's a clear record of when and how the client accepted.

Why a validity period matters more than people think

Prices don't hold indefinitely. Material costs, exchange rates, and your own capacity can all shift. A quotation without a stated validity period leaves you either honoring an outdated price or having an awkward conversation about why the number changed.

A validity period (commonly 7, 14, or 30 days) protects you from being held to a price that no longer reflects your actual costs, especially for anything involving materials priced in a volatile market. It also creates a natural, professional reason to follow up if a client hasn't responded — rather than the quote just sitting unanswered indefinitely.

The scope-of-work mistake that causes the most disputes

A quotation that says "website design — ₦300,000" invites a very different set of assumptions than one that itemizes exactly what's included: number of pages, number of revision rounds, whether hosting setup is included, and what happens if the client wants something beyond the original scope. Specificity here does more to prevent disputes than almost anything else on the document.

Turning an accepted quotation into an invoice

Once a client accepts, the invoice should mirror the quotation's line items exactly — same descriptions, same prices, same scope. A mismatch between what was quoted and what's invoiced, even an honest one, is one of the fastest ways to create friction with a client who's already mentally committed to a specific number.

Frequently asked questions

Is a quotation legally binding once a client accepts it?
Generally, yes — an accepted quotation can function as the basis of an agreement between you and the client, which is exactly why clear scope and terms matter so much on the document itself.

What's a reasonable validity period for a quotation?
This depends on your industry and how volatile your input costs are — anywhere from 7 to 30 days is common. Materials-heavy work with price-sensitive inputs often warrants a shorter window than a pure service quote.

Should I charge for preparing a detailed quotation?
For most standard business quotes, no — but for extensive custom proposals requiring significant work upfront (detailed designs, technical specifications), some businesses do charge a consultation or proposal fee. This should be communicated clearly before the work begins.

Can I revise a quotation after sending it?
Yes — if circumstances change before the client accepts, send a revised version clearly marked as such, so there's no confusion about which version is current.

What happens if the client wants extra work after accepting the quote?
This should be treated as work outside the original scope and quoted separately — this is exactly why an itemized, specific original scope matters, since it makes it clear what's "extra" versus what was already agreed.
