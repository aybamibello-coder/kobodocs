SIWES Logbook & Report Writing Guide

August 2026 · 8 min read

Every SIWES cycle, the same panic sets in around the same time: industry-based work has been happening for weeks, but the logbook is empty, the final report deadline is close, and nobody explained exactly what a "good" entry actually looks like. The SIWES coordinator's instructions are often thin on specifics, and copying a coursemate's format usually means copying their mistakes too.

Here's what actually belongs in a SIWES logbook and report, and where students consistently lose marks.

Key takeaways
  • The logbook is a daily record — specific tasks and what was learned each day, not a vague weekly summary written from memory at the end.
  • Your industry-based supervisor and school-based supervisor typically need to sign off on entries — don't leave signature collection until the very end of your attachment.
  • The final SIWES report is a separate, more structured document from the logbook, usually including an introduction to the organization, a summary of work done, skills gained, and a conclusion.
  • Vague entries ("worked on assigned tasks") are the single biggest reason logbooks get marked down — specificity is what supervisors are actually looking for.
  • Write entries as you go, not from memory weeks later — reconstructed entries are noticeably thinner and often inconsistent with dates.

What the SIWES logbook is actually for

SIWES (Students Industrial Work Experience Scheme) exists to bridge classroom learning with real industry exposure, and the logbook is the evidence that this actually happened — a running, dated record of what you did, what you learned, and how it connects to your field of study. It's assessed by both your industry-based supervisor (at the organization where you're attached) and your school-based supervisor, so it needs to satisfy both an industry and an academic audience.

What a good daily entry actually looks like

The most common mistake is writing entries too vaguely to show real learning. Compare these two:

  • Weak: "Worked on assigned tasks in the IT department."
  • Strong: "Assisted in configuring network switches for the new office wing; learned how VLANs are used to separate departmental traffic; observed troubleshooting of a connectivity issue on the 3rd floor."

The strong version does three things the weak one doesn't: it's specific about the task, it names something actually learned, and it shows engagement rather than passive presence. Every entry should aim to do at least the first two.

What to include in each entry

  • Date
  • Specific task(s) performed that day
  • Tools, software, or equipment used, if relevant
  • What was learned or observed — a new skill, a process, a concept from class seen applied in practice
  • Any challenge faced and how it was resolved (this is genuinely useful content for the final report later)
Write it the same day, not from memory later. Entries written weeks after the fact are noticeably thinner, more generic, and more likely to have date inconsistencies that a careful supervisor will notice. A short honest entry written same-day beats an elaborate one reconstructed from memory.

Getting signatures — don't leave it to the end

Your industry-based supervisor typically needs to review and sign off on your logbook periodically (weekly is common), and your school-based supervisor reviews it during scheduled visits or at the end of the attachment. Trying to collect months of unsigned entries in the final week is a common, avoidable source of stress — build signature check-ins into your routine from week one.

The final SIWES report: a different document from the logbook

The logbook is your daily working record; the final report is a separate, more polished document built from it. A typical SIWES report structure includes:

  • Introduction — brief background on SIWES itself and the organization you were attached to
  • Description of the organization — what they do, their structure, relevant departments
  • Summary of work experience — drawn from your logbook, organized by theme or department rather than a flat day-by-day repeat
  • Skills and knowledge gained — tie this explicitly back to your course of study where you can
  • Challenges encountered — and how you or the organization addressed them
  • Conclusion and recommendations

The report shouldn't just be your logbook copy-pasted into paragraphs — it's a reflective summary, and supervisors can tell the difference between a genuine synthesis and a mechanical transcription.

Common mistakes that cost marks

  • Vague, repetitive entries — "same as yesterday" style logging that shows no actual engagement.
  • Missing signatures, discovered too late to fix.
  • Gaps in the logbook with no explanation for absent days.
  • A final report that's just the logbook reformatted, rather than an actual reflective summary.
  • Generic organizational description that reads like it was copied from the company's own "About Us" page rather than written from your own observation.

Frequently asked questions

What should a SIWES logbook entry include?
The date, the specific task(s) performed, any tools or software used, and what was learned or observed — vague entries like 'worked on assigned tasks' are the most common reason logbooks get marked down.

Who needs to sign off on a SIWES logbook?
Both your industry-based supervisor (at the organization you're attached to) and your school-based supervisor typically need to review and sign the logbook, usually at regular intervals rather than all at once at the end.

Is the SIWES final report the same as the logbook?
No — the logbook is a daily working record, while the final report is a separate, more structured document that reflects on and summarizes the experience, typically including an organizational overview, skills gained, and a conclusion.

Can I write my SIWES logbook entries after the attachment ends?
It's not advisable — entries written from memory weeks later tend to be noticeably thinner and can have date inconsistencies. Writing entries the same day they happen produces a stronger, more credible logbook.

What's a common mistake in SIWES final reports?
Writing the report as a reformatted copy of the logbook rather than a genuine reflective summary — supervisors can usually tell the difference between real synthesis and mechanical transcription.
