Deed of Assignment in Nigeria: What Every Land Buyer Needs to Verify First

July 2026 · 9 min read

Land disputes are one of the most common — and most expensive — legal problems in Nigeria, and an overwhelming share of them trace back to the same root cause: someone bought land without properly verifying who actually had the right to sell it. A well-drafted Deed of Assignment matters, but it's the least risky part of the whole process. The real risk lives in what you check before you ever sign anything.

Key takeaways
  • A Deed of Assignment is the document that transfers a seller's interest in land to a buyer — but it's only as good as the seller's actual right to sell in the first place.
  • A search at the relevant Land Registry, before payment, is the single most important step in the entire transaction — it tells you whether the land is genuinely free of prior claims, government acquisition, or existing encumbrances.
  • Governor's Consent is generally required to validly transfer land held under a Certificate of Occupancy — a transaction completed without it can remain legally exposed.
  • "Omo-onile" disputes and family-land conflicts are a well-known risk in many parts of Nigeria — verifying that the person selling has genuine, undisputed authority to do so is not optional.
  • A Deed of Assignment is a starting draft for your lawyer to review, not a substitute for proper conveyancing on a transaction of this value.

What a Deed of Assignment actually is

A Deed of Assignment is the legal document that formally transfers a seller's (the assignor's) interest in a piece of land to a buyer (the assignee). It typically identifies both parties, describes the property (often referencing a survey plan), states the purchase price, and confirms that the assignor is transferring their full interest and title to the assignee. It's the document you'll ultimately need for subsequent registration and, where applicable, for obtaining Governor's Consent.

Why title verification matters more than the document

The document doesn't create the right to sell — it only records it. A beautifully drafted Deed of Assignment is worthless if the person signing it didn't actually have valid title to transfer. Verification comes before drafting, not after.

Before any money changes hands, a search at the relevant state Land Registry is essential — this confirms whether the land is registered, whether there are existing encumbrances (a mortgage, a prior sale, a pending legal dispute), and whether the property has been subject to government acquisition. Skipping this step is how buyers end up purchasing land that was already sold to someone else, or land the government had already acquired for public use.

Governor's Consent — why it matters for C of O land

Under Nigerian land law, land held under a Certificate of Occupancy (C of O) generally requires the Governor's Consent before it can be validly assigned to another party. A transaction completed without obtaining this consent can leave the buyer's title legally vulnerable, even where a Deed of Assignment was properly signed and the purchase price fully paid. This is a step that's sometimes skipped to save time or cost — doing so trades a real, ongoing legal risk for a short-term convenience.

Family land and "omo-onile" disputes

In several parts of Nigeria, land disputes frequently arise from family-owned or communally-held land, where one family member sells land without the full authority or agreement of the rest of the family, or where informal land-owning groups ("omo-onile") reassert claims after a sale. Verifying that the seller has clear, undisputed, and complete authority to sell — not just partial or contested authority — is one of the most important checks a buyer can make, and often requires more than a quick conversation; it can mean requesting family consent documentation or a broader search into the land's history.

What proper due diligence looks like

  • Land Registry search — confirms registration status and any existing encumbrances.
  • Physical inspection of the property — confirms boundaries match the survey plan and there's no existing occupant or competing claim.
  • Verification of the seller's identity and authority — especially critical where the seller is representing a family or a company rather than selling as an individual owner.
  • Survey plan verification — confirms the plotted boundaries are accurate and consistent with official records.
  • Engaging a lawyer for the transaction — for something of this value, professional legal review isn't an optional extra.

What a Deed of Assignment should include

  • Full details of both the assignor (seller) and assignee (buyer).
  • A clear description of the property, referencing the survey plan/plot number.
  • The purchase price and confirmation of payment.
  • A statement that the assignor has the right, title, and authority to transfer the property.
  • Witness attestation, and space for Governor's Consent endorsement where applicable.

Frequently asked questions

Is a Deed of Assignment enough on its own to prove ownership?
No — it needs to be supported by proper title verification and, where required, registration and Governor's Consent. The document alone doesn't establish that the seller had valid title to transfer.

What's the difference between a C of O and a Deed of Assignment?
A Certificate of Occupancy is the government-issued document establishing a right of occupancy over land. A Deed of Assignment is the document used to transfer an existing interest in land from one party to another — the two serve different purposes in the overall chain of title.

Can I skip the Land Registry search if the seller seems trustworthy?
This isn't advisable given how much is at stake — trustworthiness isn't a substitute for verifying the actual state of the title, and many land disputes involve sellers who were themselves unaware of a competing claim.

Do I need a lawyer if I'm just buying a small residential plot?
Given the value typically involved and the legal complexity of land transactions in Nigeria, professional legal review is worth the cost regardless of the plot's size.

What happens if I complete a purchase without Governor's Consent?
The transaction can remain legally incomplete or vulnerable, even with a signed Deed of Assignment and full payment. It's worth pursuing consent as part of completing the transaction properly, rather than treating it as optional paperwork.
