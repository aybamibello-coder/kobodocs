Offer, Appointment & Resignation Letters in Nigeria: A Practical Guide

August 2026 · 9 min read

Every employment relationship in Nigeria runs through a handful of letters — and most small business owners either skip them, reuse a template that's missing something important, or mix up which letter is for which stage. Employees searching for the right wording to resign professionally hit the same confusion from the other side.

This guide covers the three letters that actually matter — offer, appointment, and resignation — what belongs in each, and where people commonly get them wrong.

Key takeaways
  • An offer letter proposes employment; an appointment letter confirms it's finalized — they're not interchangeable, and skipping straight to appointment without an offer step can cause disputes.
  • Under the Labour Act, written particulars of employment should reach an employee within a defined period of starting — verbal-only agreements leave both sides exposed.
  • A resignation letter doesn't need to explain why you're leaving — a professional, brief letter with a clear last working day is all that's required.
  • Notice periods should follow what's stated in the employment contract or appointment letter — check that first before assuming a default.
  • Keeping signed copies of every stage — offer, appointment, and any resignation correspondence — protects both employer and employee if a dispute ever comes up.

Offer letter vs. appointment letter — they're not the same thing

These get used interchangeably in casual conversation, but they serve different legal purposes:

  • An offer letter is a proposal — "we'd like to offer you this role, on these terms" — usually sent before all details (start date, sometimes salary specifics) are fully locked down. The candidate can still negotiate or decline at this stage.
  • An appointment letter is sent once the offer has been accepted and finalized — it confirms the employment relationship formally, with complete terms, and is typically what both parties sign as the actual start of the employment record.

Some smaller employers skip the offer stage entirely and go straight to an appointment letter — this works fine when terms were already agreed verbally or over email, but it removes a useful buffer stage where terms can still be adjusted before anything is formally signed.

What an offer letter should include

  • Job title and a brief description of the role
  • Proposed start date
  • Compensation — salary, and any allowances that are relevant to the offer
  • Reporting line — who the person will report to
  • Any conditions the offer depends on (reference checks, certificate verification, a probation period)
  • A deadline for the candidate to respond by

What an appointment letter should include

This is the more formal, complete document, and under the Labour Act, employees are entitled to written particulars of their employment within a set period of starting work — so this isn't just good practice, it's a legal expectation. A proper appointment letter covers:

  • Full job title, department, and reporting structure
  • Confirmed start date
  • Full compensation breakdown — basic salary, housing, transport, and any other allowances (this also matters for correct PAYE calculation down the line)
  • Working hours and work location
  • Probation period, if any, and what happens at the end of it
  • Leave entitlement
  • Notice period required to end the employment, from either side
  • Any specific policies the employee is expected to follow (confidentiality, conduct)
  • Signature lines for both employer and employee
Why the compensation breakdown matters: having basic, housing, and transport itemized separately — not just a single lump salary figure — is what makes pension (8% of basic + housing + transport) and payroll tax calculations accurate later. A single undifferentiated salary number causes real problems for payroll further down the line.

Probation periods: what's typical

There's no single statutory probation length that applies to every role in Nigeria — it's set by the employer and stated in the appointment letter, commonly somewhere between three and six months depending on the seniority and nature of the role. What matters most is that the appointment letter states clearly what happens at the end of probation — confirmation, extension, or termination — rather than leaving it ambiguous.

Writing a resignation letter

On the other end of the employment relationship, a resignation letter is one of the simplest professional documents to write — and one of the easiest to overcomplicate. It doesn't need to explain your reasons for leaving, doesn't need to be lengthy, and shouldn't be used to air grievances, regardless of how the job went. A good resignation letter needs only:

  • A clear statement that you're resigning
  • Your last working day, calculated from your required notice period
  • A brief note of thanks (optional, but standard practice and good for your professional record)
  • Your signature and date

Notice periods: check your contract first

How much notice you owe (or are owed) depends on what's stated in your employment or appointment letter — this varies significantly by role, seniority, and employer, so there's no universal default to rely on. If nothing was specified in writing, the Labour Act provides fallback minimums based on length of service, but these are meant as a backstop, not a substitute for checking your actual contract first.

Common mistakes on both sides

  • No written appointment letter at all — a verbal agreement or a casual email thread leaves both employer and employee exposed if there's ever a dispute about terms.
  • Salary given as one lump figure instead of broken into basic, housing, and transport — this causes downstream payroll and pension calculation problems.
  • Resignation letters that explain too much — venting frustrations in writing rarely helps and can complicate a reference request later.
  • No clear notice period stated in the appointment letter, leaving both sides guessing when a resignation happens.
  • Backdating letters to paper over a gap — this creates a genuinely risky, inconsistent paper trail if the employment relationship is ever formally reviewed.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between an offer letter and an appointment letter in Nigeria?
An offer letter proposes employment and can still be negotiated or declined. An appointment letter confirms the employment is finalized, with complete terms, and is typically what both parties sign to formally start the employment record.

Does a resignation letter need to state a reason for leaving?
No. A professional resignation letter only needs to state that you're resigning and your last working day — explaining your reasons isn't required and is often best left out.

How much notice am I required to give when resigning in Nigeria?
This depends on what's stated in your employment or appointment letter — check that first. If nothing was specified in writing, the Labour Act provides fallback minimum notice periods based on length of service.

Is a written appointment letter legally required in Nigeria?
Under the Labour Act, employees are entitled to written particulars of their employment within a defined period of starting work — so while enforcement varies, a written appointment letter is the legally expected standard, not just good practice.

Should salary be listed as one figure or broken down in an appointment letter?
It should be broken down — basic salary, housing allowance, and transport allowance listed separately, since this breakdown is what payroll uses to correctly calculate pension and PAYE tax later.
