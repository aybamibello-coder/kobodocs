What a Bank or BOI Loan Officer Actually Wants to See in Your Business Plan

August 2026 · 7 min read

A lot of business plans read well and still get rejected. The prose is fine, the mission statement is clear, the market description sounds reasonable — and then a loan officer flips to the financial section and finds numbers that don't add up, aren't itemized, or clearly weren't stress-tested against reality. Getting the narrative right matters less than most people assume; getting the numbers defensible matters more.

Key takeaways
  • Vague or rounded numbers ("about ₦2 million to start") read as guessed, not calculated — itemize everything.
  • A break-even point is one of the first things a loan officer looks for, and its absence is a red flag.
  • Use of funds needs to be specific enough that the lender can picture exactly where the money goes.
  • Shorter and specific beats long and generic — reviewers read many of these.

The numbers matter more than the writing

It's tempting to spend most of the effort on the narrative sections — mission, market description, competitive advantage — because they're the easiest to write. But a loan officer or grant reviewer, especially one looking at dozens of applications, tends to jump straight to the financials to sanity-check the request before reading the story around it. If the startup costs are rounded to the nearest million with no breakdown, or the revenue forecast jumps in a way that isn't explained, that's often enough to end serious consideration regardless of how well the rest reads.

What "itemized" actually means

"₦3,000,000 for startup costs" is a guess dressed up as a number. "₦1,500,000 equipment, ₦800,000 rent deposit, ₦1,200,000 initial inventory, ₦300,000 marketing and launch, ₦200,000 registration and legal" is a plan. The second version doesn't just look more credible — it forces the business owner to actually think through what the money is for, which surfaces gaps (forgotten costs, unrealistic estimates) before a lender finds them.

The break-even point is not optional

This is one of the most commonly missing pieces, and one of the first things an experienced reviewer looks for. It answers a simple question: at what point does this business stop losing money and start covering its own costs? Without it, a lender has no way to judge whether the revenue projections are realistic or just optimistic — a break-even calculation forces the revenue forecast and the expense forecast to actually connect to each other.

A common mistake: revenue projections with no visible growth logic. Jumping from ₦500,000 in month one to ₦3,000,000 in month six with no explanation reads as wishful thinking. A stated, consistent monthly growth assumption (even a modest one) that a reader can trace month by month is far more convincing than an impressive-looking final number with no path shown to get there.

Use of funds: be specific about where the money actually goes

"Business expansion" is not a use of funds a lender can evaluate. "Securing a 2-year lease on retail space in Ikeja, stocking three months of inventory, and covering the first two months of staff salaries while sales ramp up" is something a lender can actually assess for risk and reasonableness. The more specifically the request maps to concrete, verifiable costs, the more it reads as a plan rather than a hope.

What's different about BOI, NIRSAL, and other government-backed funds

The core of what makes a plan credible — real numbers, a clear break-even, specific use of funds — applies whether you're applying to a commercial bank or a government-backed fund like the Bank of Industry (BOI) or NIRSAL. What tends to differ is emphasis: government and development funds often weight job creation, alignment with their specific sector priorities, and formal business registration status more heavily than a commercial bank might. Checking a specific fund's stated priorities before finalizing the plan is worth the extra half hour — tailoring which points get emphasized rarely means changing the underlying numbers.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need my business registered before applying for a loan or grant?
Requirements vary by lender and fund, but formal registration (even as a Business Name, not necessarily a full limited company) is commonly expected or strongly preferred. Confirming the specific requirement for your target lender before applying saves a wasted submission.

How far out should revenue projections go?
Twelve months is the standard expectation for most Nigerian lenders and funds — long enough to show a realistic growth trajectory, short enough to stay grounded rather than speculative.

Can I reuse the same business plan for multiple lenders?
The core financials and structure can stay the same, but it's worth adjusting the emphasis and any specific context sections to match what each particular lender or fund says they prioritize.

Build a bank-and-grant-ready plan with real startup costs, a 12-month forecast, and an automatic break-even calculation — ₦10,000, one-time.
