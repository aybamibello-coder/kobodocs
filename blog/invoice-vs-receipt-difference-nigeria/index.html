Invoice vs Receipt: What's the Difference?

August 2026 · 6 min read

"Just send me an invoice" and "send me a receipt" get used interchangeably by a lot of customers — and a lot of small business owners issue whichever one they're more familiar with, regardless of which one the situation actually calls for. The two documents prove completely different things, and using the wrong one can cause real problems, especially around tax records and payment disputes.

Key takeaways
  • An invoice is a request for payment, sent before or at the point of a sale — it says "you owe this."
  • A receipt is proof that payment was already made — it says "this has been paid."
  • You typically need both for a single transaction: the invoice to request payment, the receipt to confirm it landed.
  • For tax and bookkeeping purposes, the receipt (not the invoice) is what proves income was actually received.
  • Issuing a "receipt" for money you haven't actually received yet is a common and risky mistake — it misrepresents the transaction's real status.

The core difference in one line

An invoice asks for money. A receipt confirms money was received. That's the entire distinction — everything else follows from it.

What an invoice does

An invoice is issued before or at the point of a sale, itemizing what's being sold, the amount owed, payment terms, and a due date. It's a formal request for payment — the customer's copy of "here's what you owe and by when." For services or goods delivered on credit terms, the invoice is often the only document exchanged until payment actually happens.

A good invoice includes: your business details, the client's details, an invoice number, itemized description of goods/services, amount (with VAT/WHT if applicable), due date, and payment instructions. Our invoice guide covers this in full detail.

What a receipt does

A receipt is issued after payment has actually been received — it's proof of a completed transaction, not a request. It typically includes the amount paid, the date paid, the payment method, and what it was for. For the customer, it's their proof of payment if a dispute ever comes up. For the business, it's a core bookkeeping record of actual income received.

When you need both

For most sales involving any delay between the sale and payment — invoicing a client who pays in 30 days, a deposit followed by a balance payment, credit terms with a regular customer — you need both documents at different points in the same transaction: the invoice to request payment, and the receipt once it actually arrives, confirming the invoice has been settled.

For an instant cash-and-carry sale — someone buys and pays on the spot — a receipt alone is often sufficient, since there's no gap between the request and the payment to document separately.

The mistake that causes real problems: issuing a "receipt" before payment

This happens more than you'd expect — a business issues something labeled "receipt" as a formality when a sale is agreed, even though the money hasn't landed yet. This is a genuine problem, not just a technicality: a receipt is meant to be proof that payment happened, and issuing one before it actually has misrepresents the transaction's real status. If the customer then doesn't pay, you're left with a document on record that says they did. Use an invoice for anything that hasn't been paid yet, no matter how confident you are the payment is coming.

Why this matters for bookkeeping specifically

When you're tracking income for your own records (or eventually, for tax purposes), what matters is money actually received — which is what a receipt documents, not an invoice. An outstanding invoice represents expected income, not yet-realized income; counting it as revenue before it's actually paid can make your books look healthier than your actual cash position really is. This is exactly the same distinction behind Days Sales Outstanding (DSO) tracking for businesses managing receivables — see our receivable management guide if outstanding invoices are a recurring issue for your business.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main difference between an invoice and a receipt?
An invoice is a request for payment, sent before or at the point of sale. A receipt is proof that payment has already been received. One asks for money; the other confirms it arrived.

Do I need to issue both an invoice and a receipt for one sale?
For sales with any gap between the sale and payment — credit terms, deposits, invoiced clients — yes, you typically issue the invoice first to request payment, then the receipt once it's actually paid. For an instant cash sale, a receipt alone is often enough.

Is it okay to issue a receipt before payment is received?
No — a receipt is meant to prove payment actually happened. Issuing one before the money has landed misrepresents the transaction and creates a record that doesn't match reality if the payment doesn't come through as expected.

Which document proves income for bookkeeping purposes — an invoice or a receipt?
A receipt, since it documents money actually received. An unpaid invoice represents expected income, not realized income, and shouldn't be counted the same way in your books.

Can one document serve as both an invoice and a receipt?
Not accurately — they represent different transaction states (owed vs. paid). Some businesses mark an invoice as 'paid' after the fact rather than issuing a separate receipt, which can work for simple records, but a proper receipt remains the clearer standalone proof of payment.
