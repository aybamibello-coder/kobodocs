Next of Kin Form: What Banks & Employers Actually Require

August 2026 · 4 min read

It's one of the most common forms Nigerians fill out — opening a bank account, starting a new job, NYSC registration, or moving into a hostel — and also one of the most frequently rushed, filled in with a name and phone number scribbled on whatever paper is handed over. It's a short document, but a few details are worth getting right.

Key takeaways
  • Requirements vary slightly by institution — a bank, an employer, NYSC, and a hostel may each want slightly different fields.
  • Full name, relationship, phone number, and address are the near-universal core requirements.
  • An illegible or incomplete form is one of the most common reasons these get sent back for correction.

What's typically required

Across most contexts, a next of kin form asks for the same core details: the next of kin's full legal name, their relationship to the applicant (parent, sibling, spouse, etc.), a working phone number, and a current address. Some institutions — particularly banks and some employers — also ask for a passport photograph of the next of kin, and occasionally a signature.

Where the specifics differ

Banks generally want this on file in case the account holder can't be reached for something important — the requirements tend to be straightforward but strictly enforced (a phone number that doesn't ring when tested during account opening can cause delays).

Employers often use it for emergency contact purposes and sometimes for benefits or next-of-kin payout purposes, so accuracy matters more here than it might first appear.

NYSC registration has its own specific format as part of the broader documentation package, usually processed alongside other NYSC paperwork rather than as a standalone form.

Hostel or school admissions commonly request this for emergency contact purposes during a student's time away from home.

Common mistakes worth avoiding

  • An outdated phone number. A next of kin form is meaningless if the number on it doesn't work when someone actually needs to use it.
  • Illegible handwriting on a handwritten form. A form that has to be sent back for clarification adds delay to whatever process it's part of.
  • Leaving the relationship field vague. "Family" or "relative" is less useful than "elder sister" or "father" — some institutions specifically require a defined relationship.

Frequently asked questions

Who can be listed as a next of kin?
Typically a close family member — a parent, sibling, spouse, or adult child — though the specific institution requesting the form sets its own rules on who qualifies.

Can I list more than one next of kin?
Some forms allow a primary and secondary contact; others only have space for one. Check the specific form's fields rather than assuming.

Can I update it later if my next of kin changes?
Usually yes — most institutions allow updating these details by submitting a new form or a formal request, though the exact process depends on the institution.
