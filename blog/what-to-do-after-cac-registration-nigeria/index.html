What to Do After Registering Your Business in Nigeria

August 2026 · 7 min read

Getting the CAC certificate feels like the finish line — but for a registered company (as opposed to a simple business name), it's actually the starting line for a short list of paperwork that most new founders don't find out about until a bank or a partner asks for it. This is the checklist nobody hands you at the CAC office.

Key takeaways
  • A registered company (Ltd) needs a Board Resolution before a corporate bank account can typically be opened — this is one of the most common hold-ups new founders hit.
  • Share Certificates are the legal proof of ownership for each shareholder — without them, ownership exists on paper at CAC but isn't formally evidenced to the shareholders themselves.
  • Nigerian company law requires certain statutory registers to be kept — Register of Members, Register of Directors — even for a small, newly incorporated company.
  • A business name registration has a shorter post-registration checklist than a full Ltd incorporation — know which one you actually did.
  • None of this paperwork needs to be expensive or slow if you know exactly what's required upfront, rather than discovering each requirement one at a time.

First: which one did you actually register?

This checklist is for a registered limited liability company (Ltd) specifically — a business name registration is a simpler structure with a much shorter list of follow-up requirements (mainly tax registration and, depending on your bank, a business account). If you're not sure which one you have, check your CAC certificate: a Ltd certificate will show an RC number and reference "Limited," while a business name shows a BN number.

1. Board Resolution

Most Nigerian banks will ask for a Board Resolution before opening a corporate account for a newly incorporated company — a formal document showing the directors have resolved to open the account and naming who's authorized to operate it (sign cheques, make transfers, etc.). This is one of the most common places new founders get stuck: they have the CAC certificate, go to open an account, and get told they need a document they've never heard of. Sorting this out before you walk into the bank saves a wasted trip.

2. Share Certificates

Incorporation at CAC establishes who the shareholders are on the public record, but each shareholder should also be issued their own Share Certificate — the actual legal document proving their ownership stake, which they should hold onto. This matters more than it might seem for a small company: if a shareholder ever needs to prove ownership (selling their shares, resolving a dispute, or just for their own records), a share certificate is the standard evidence — not just "the CAC records say so."

3. Statutory registers

Nigerian company law requires certain registers to be maintained, even for a small, newly incorporated business:

  • Register of Members — who the shareholders are and their shareholdings
  • Register of Directors — who the directors are and their particulars

These aren't just formalities — they're what a regulator, auditor, or potential investor will expect to see if they ever review the company's records, and not having them in order can cause real friction at exactly the moment you need things to move quickly (due diligence for an investment, a loan application, a dispute).

4. Tax registration

Beyond the incorporation itself, a newly registered company needs to register for tax purposes — this is a separate step from CAC incorporation, not an automatic part of it. Requirements and specifics are worth confirming with the relevant tax authority directly, since this is an area where getting current, accurate guidance matters more than a general summary.

5. Corporate bank account

With the Board Resolution and other requested documents in hand (typically the CAC certificate, Memorandum and Articles of Association, and valid ID for the signatories), opening a corporate account becomes a much smoother process than showing up with just the certificate alone.

Common mistakes founders make right after incorporation

  • Going to the bank without a Board Resolution and having to make a second trip once they find out it's needed.
  • Never issuing share certificates to shareholders, leaving ownership formally undocumented at the individual level.
  • Not maintaining statutory registers from day one, then scrambling to reconstruct them later when a bank, auditor, or investor asks.
  • Assuming CAC incorporation automatically handles tax registration — it doesn't; that's a separate step.
  • Mixing up business name and Ltd company requirements — they're genuinely different structures with different follow-up paperwork.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a Board Resolution to open a business bank account in Nigeria?
Most banks require one for a newly incorporated limited liability company before opening a corporate account — it's a formal document showing the directors have resolved to open the account and naming who's authorized to operate it.

What's the difference in post-registration requirements between a business name and a Ltd company?
A business name has a shorter checklist, mainly tax registration and a business account. A registered Ltd company has additional requirements — a Board Resolution, Share Certificates, and statutory registers like the Register of Members and Register of Directors.

Do I need to issue Share Certificates even for a small company?
Yes — CAC incorporation records who the shareholders are, but each shareholder should still be issued their own Share Certificate as the actual legal proof of their ownership stake, which matters if ownership is ever questioned, sold, or reviewed.

Is tax registration automatic after CAC incorporation?
No — tax registration is a separate step from company incorporation, not something that happens automatically when you get your CAC certificate.

What registers is a Nigerian company legally required to keep?
At minimum, a Register of Members (shareholders and their shareholdings) and a Register of Directors (directors and their particulars) — required under Nigerian company law even for small, newly incorporated companies.
