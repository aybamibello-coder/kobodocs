How to Write a Grant Proposal Donors Actually Fund

August 2026 · 7 min read

Most grant proposals that get rejected aren't rejected because the cause wasn't worthwhile — they're rejected because a reviewer couldn't tell, from the document itself, exactly what would happen with the money and how anyone would know if it worked. A compelling mission statement gets attention; a clear, measurable plan is what actually gets funded.

Key takeaways
  • Vague outcomes ("improve livelihoods") get rejected far more often than modest, measurable ones ("40 households with increased monthly income, verified by follow-up survey").
  • A logframe — mapping goal, outcomes, outputs, and activities with indicators for each — is the tool most donors and reviewers actually look for.
  • A budget needs to visibly connect to the activities it funds, not just list totals by category.
  • Government funds (BOI, SMEDAN, CBN-AGSMEIS) and private/international donors evaluate proposals similarly at the core, though emphasis differs.

Why "improve livelihoods" gets rejected

It's an understandable instinct to describe a project's impact in broad, aspirational terms — it sounds ambitious and worthwhile. But a reviewer reading dozens of proposals needs something they can actually assess: what specifically will change, for how many people, and how will you know it happened? "Improve livelihoods" tells a reviewer nothing they can verify. "40 households in [community] show increased monthly income within 6 months, verified by a follow-up survey" tells them exactly what to expect and how success will be checked.

The logframe, explained simply

A logframe (logical framework) is the tool most donors use to assess whether a project's logic actually holds together — whether the activities genuinely lead to the outputs, which genuinely lead to the outcome, which genuinely serves the goal. It's usually presented as a table with four levels, each needing an indicator (how you'll measure it) and a means of verification (how you'll prove it).

LevelWhat it meansExample
Goal / ImpactThe long-term change your project contributes toReduced youth unemployment in [LGA]
OutcomeThe specific change your project directly causes60 trained youth secure employment or start a business within 6 months
OutputsThe concrete deliverables your activities produce60 youth complete a 4-week skills training program
ActivitiesWhat you actually doRecruit trainees, deliver training sessions, provide starter toolkits

Each level should logically cause the one above it — if you can't explain how your activities produce your stated outputs, or how your outputs lead to your outcome, that's usually a sign the plan needs tightening before it goes to a reviewer, not after.

Not every application asks for a formal logframe table — but even when one isn't explicitly required, thinking through a proposal in this structure (goal, outcome, outputs, activities, each with a way to measure it) tends to produce a stronger, more fundable proposal regardless of the exact format requested.

Budgets that connect to the plan

A budget that just lists "Personnel: ₦2,000,000, Materials: ₦1,500,000, Logistics: ₦800,000" tells a reviewer the totals but not whether they're reasonable for the stated activities. Breaking it down by activity — how much of the personnel budget covers the training delivery specifically, how much covers program coordination — lets a reviewer sanity-check the numbers against the plan, which builds confidence rather than requiring blind trust in a lump sum.

Government funds vs. private and international donors

The underlying discipline — measurable outcomes, a logical structure, a connected budget — applies whether you're applying to an international donor, a corporate CSR program, or a government fund like BOI, SMEDAN, or the CBN-AGSMEIS scheme. What tends to shift is emphasis: government-backed funds often weight job creation numbers and alignment with specific national priorities more heavily, while private and international donors may focus more on sustainability and community ownership beyond the funding period. Reading a specific call's stated priorities before finalizing the proposal is worth the time — the core structure rarely needs to change, just what gets emphasized.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a grant proposal be?
Follow whatever the specific call for proposals asks for — donors vary widely, from a two-page concept note to a full multi-section application. Padding beyond what's requested rarely helps.

Do I need a registered NGO to apply for grants in Nigeria?
Requirements vary by funder. Many international donors and government funds require formal registration (as an NGO, cooperative, or business), so it's worth confirming the specific funder's eligibility criteria before investing time in a full proposal.

Can one proposal be reused for multiple funding calls?
The core project logic and logframe can often be reused, but budgets, specific language, and emphasis usually need adjusting to match each funder's stated priorities and format.
