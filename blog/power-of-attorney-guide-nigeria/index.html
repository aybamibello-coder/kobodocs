Power of Attorney in Nigeria: Types, Format & When You Actually Need One

August 2026 · 10 min read

A Power of Attorney (POA) lets someone else act on your behalf — sign documents, manage property, handle a transaction — when you can't be there yourself. It's one of the most useful legal documents in everyday Nigerian life, whether you're abroad and need someone to handle a property sale back home, or you're temporarily unavailable and need a family member to act for you at the bank or with a government agency.

It's also one of the most misunderstood. This guide covers what a POA actually does, the difference between the types, and how to make one that will actually be accepted.

Key takeaways
  • A Power of Attorney authorizes someone (your "attorney" or agent) to act on your behalf — it doesn't transfer ownership of anything.
  • A General POA covers broad authority; a Special POA is limited to one specific transaction or purpose — most situations call for the special kind.
  • To be valid for land or property transactions, a POA generally needs to be signed, witnessed, and — for real estate — registered.
  • A POA can be revoked at any time by the person who granted it, as long as they're still of sound mind.
  • It automatically ends if the person who granted it dies or loses mental capacity, unless it's specifically drafted as "durable" or "enduring."

What a Power of Attorney actually does

A POA is a written authorization: you (the "donor" or principal) give someone else (the "donee" or attorney) the legal right to act in your name for a defined purpose. It doesn't give away ownership of anything — if you grant someone POA over your house, you still own the house; they just have the legal standing to sign documents, collect rent, or complete a sale on your behalf, within whatever limits the document sets.

This distinction matters because it's the most common misunderstanding: a POA is a delegation of authority, not a transfer of rights. The donee acts as your agent, not as a new owner.

General vs. Special Power of Attorney

The two forms are not interchangeable, and using the wrong one is a common source of problems:

  • General Power of Attorney gives broad authority to act across many kinds of transactions — managing property, running a business, handling financial affairs generally. Because it's so broad, banks, courts, and government agencies are often cautious about accepting it, and it carries more risk if the person you appoint misuses it.
  • Special (or Specific) Power of Attorney is limited to one clearly defined transaction or purpose — selling a specific property, collecting a specific document, representing you in a single transaction. This is what most people actually need, and it's far more readily accepted because the scope is unambiguous.
Rule of thumb: if you can describe exactly what you need done in one sentence — "sell my land at [address]," "collect my certificate of occupancy from [agency]" — a Special POA is almost always the right choice.

When Nigerians actually use a Power of Attorney

  • Diaspora Nigerians managing property back home — the single most common use case. A relative or trusted associate is given a Special POA to sell, lease, or manage a property while the owner is abroad.
  • Land and property transactions — where the seller can't be physically present for every stage of a sale.
  • Business transactions — authorizing someone to sign contracts or represent the company in a specific deal while a director or owner is unavailable.
  • Banking and financial matters — though many Nigerian banks have their own internal mandate forms and may not accept a general POA for account access; check with the specific bank first.
  • Court and legal proceedings — authorizing a representative to act in a legal matter on your behalf.

What makes a Power of Attorney valid

There's no single national "POA Act" — Power of Attorney in Nigeria is governed by common law principles and, for land-related POAs specifically, by state Land Instruments Registration laws. In practice, for a POA to hold up:

  • It must be in writing and clearly state the donor, the donee, and the exact scope of authority granted.
  • It must be signed by the donor — the person granting the authority.
  • It should be witnessed, typically by at least one witness who isn't a party to the document.
  • For land or property transactions specifically, it generally needs to be registered at the relevant state Lands Registry to be valid against third parties — an unregistered POA for a property deal can create real problems later.   • Notarization is often recommended, and required by some institutions or when the POA is being used across international borders (e.g., a Nigerian abroad granting POA over Nigerian property).

What a Power of Attorney should include

  • Full legal names and addresses of both the donor and the donee
  • A clear, specific description of what the donee is authorized to do
  • Any limits on that authority — dates, monetary limits, specific properties or transactions
  • The date it takes effect, and when (if ever) it expires
  • Signature of the donor, and witness details

Revoking a Power of Attorney

A POA isn't permanent by default, and the donor can revoke it at any time, provided they're still of sound mind. To revoke one properly: put the revocation in writing, notify the donee directly, and — critically, if the original POA was used in any registered transaction (like property) — notify any third parties (banks, Lands Registry, business partners) who may have relied on it, so they know it's no longer valid. Simply telling the donee isn't enough if others were relying on the original document.

When a Power of Attorney automatically ends

  • The donor dies
  • The donor loses mental capacity (unless the POA is specifically drafted to survive this — an "enduring" or "durable" POA, which needs to say so explicitly)
  • The purpose is completed (for a Special POA tied to one transaction)
  • The stated expiry date passes, if one was set
  • It's formally revoked by the donor

Power of Attorney vs. Will — don't confuse the two

These serve completely different purposes and people mix them up often. A Power of Attorney is about someone acting for you while you're alive — it becomes void the moment you die. A Will only takes effect after you die, and governs how your estate is distributed. If you're thinking about both — say, you want someone to manage your affairs now and also decide what happens to your estate later — you need two separate documents, not one.

Common mistakes that cause a POA to be rejected

  • Vague scope — "to handle my affairs" is too broad and often gets challenged; be specific about what's authorized.
  • Missing witness signatures.
  • Using a General POA for a land transaction where the receiving institution specifically wants a Special POA scoped to that property.
  • Failing to register a property-related POA at the state Lands Registry.
  • No clear expiry or end condition, leaving the scope of authority ambiguous over time.

Frequently asked questions

Does a Power of Attorney need to be notarized in Nigeria?
Notarization isn't always a strict legal requirement, but it's commonly recommended and often required by banks, government agencies, or when the document will be used internationally — check with the specific institution you'll be presenting it to.

Can a Power of Attorney be used to sell land in Nigeria?
Yes, this is one of the most common uses, particularly for diaspora Nigerians — but the POA generally needs to be registered at the relevant state Lands Registry to be valid for a property transaction, and a Special POA scoped to that specific property is usually expected.

Can I cancel a Power of Attorney after signing it?
Yes, as long as you're of sound mind, you can revoke a Power of Attorney at any time by putting the revocation in writing and notifying the person you appointed, along with any third parties who were relying on the original document.

What happens to a Power of Attorney if the donor dies?
It automatically becomes void. A Power of Attorney only authorizes someone to act on your behalf while you're alive — after death, a Will (or the rules of intestacy, if there's no Will) governs what happens instead.

Is a General Power of Attorney better than a Special one?
Not usually — a Special Power of Attorney, scoped to one clear transaction, is generally preferred because it's easier for banks, courts, and registries to accept, and it limits your risk if the person you appointed doesn't act in your interest.
